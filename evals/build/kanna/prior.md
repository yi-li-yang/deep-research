# Prior: Japanese hand planes (kanna): makers, steels and setup

_Written by a fresh agent with no tools on 2026-10-06, as step 1 of the skill. Training-only beliefs. Its self-reported detail on this niche is thickest through about 2023–24._

## Anatomy, types, vocabulary
- [high] A kanna is pulled. Its laminated blade (kanna-mi) is held by friction in an oak body (dai), between the bed and two side grooves (osae-mizo). There is no wedge, lever cap, frog or screw adjuster.
- [low] The blade tapers in thickness, thicker toward the head (taper figures unknown).
- [high] All adjustment is done with a hammer (genno): tap the head (kashira) to advance, tap the back of the dai (daijiri) to retract.
- [high] Two-blade planes (nimai-ganna) have a chipbreaker (uragane) under a pin (osae-bō); single-blade planes (ichimai-ganna) have none.
- [medium] The bench plane (dai-ganna) replaced the yari-ganna and chōna around the 15th–16th century; the chipbreaker arrived in the Meiji era.
- [high] Blades are two layers: hard steel (hagane) forge-welded to soft iron (jigane), with a hollow back (ura-suki) and a narrow flat strip (ito-ura).
- [medium] Bed angle "8-sun kōbai" (≈38.7°); 7.5 sun for soft conifers; 9–10 sun (≈42–45°) for hardwoods.
- [medium] Sun-pachi (寸八) is the standard smoother, with a blade ≈70 mm wide. Sun-roku ≈65 mm, sun-yon ≈60 mm and ni-sun ≈80 mm, so the name is not the modern width.
- [medium] Grades and types: ara-, naka- and jō-shiko or shiage (rough to finish); kiwa, shakuri, wakitori, mentori, sori-dai, uchi-maru and soto-maru, dai-naoshi.
- [medium] Dai wood is white oak (shirakashi) or red oak (akagashi), sometimes isu; air-dried for years.

## Steels
- [high] Quality blades mostly use YSS Shirogami and Aogami; Hitachi Metals became Proterial in Jan 2023 after a Bain-led buyout.
- [medium] Shirogami #1/#2/#3 carbon ≈1.25–1.35/1.05–1.15/0.8–0.9%. Aogami adds Cr and W. Aogami Super has ≈1.4–1.5% C plus Mo and V.
- [medium] Lore: Shirogami takes the keenest edge for finishing softwoods; Aogami lasts longer in hardwoods. Heat treatment matters more than the label.
- [medium] Hand-forged blades are about HRC 62–65; very hard Shirogami can chip on knots.
- [medium] Old wrought iron jigane is prized; modern blades use gokunantetsu or factory-clad stock.
- [medium] Tamahagane comes from the NBTHK's Nittōho tatara in Shimane; tamahagane kanna are rare and expensive.
- [low] "Swedish steel" kanna have a cult reputation; much of that stock is old.
- [medium] Non-traditional options exist: HSS, powder steel, carbide, and replaceable-blade (kaeba-shiki) planes.

## Makers and trade
- [high] Chiyozuru Korehide (1874–1957) is the most revered historical smith.
- [medium] Production centers: Miki (Hyōgo, the largest), Yoita and Sanjō (Niigata), historic Tokyo smiths.
- [medium] A plane is a joint product: smith (kaji) + dai maker (dai-ya) + fitting (shikomi).
- [medium] The stamped name (mei) is often a brand or dealer name; famous names are reused.
- [low] Revered 20th-century names: Usui Kengo ("Kiyotada"), the Ishidō line, Iwasaki Shigeyoshi, Tasai.
- [low] Current retail names: Tsunesaburō (Miki), Kiyohisa, Funahiro, Chiyozuru Sadahide, Kunihiro (status unknown).
- [medium] Mass-market Kakuri and SK11; many cheap "Japanese" planes are made in China or Taiwan.
- [medium] Smiths are aging with few apprentices; carpenter demand is down while hobbyist and overseas demand is up.
- [medium] Sellers: Hida Tool, Japan Woodworker, Tools from Japan, Mokuchi Studio (US); Lee Valley (CA); Dictum, Fine Tools (DE); Workshop Heaven, Classic Hand Tools (UK); Yahoo! Auctions via proxy.
- [medium] References: Odate (1984); Covington; Wilbur Pan; Chris Hall; Yann Giguère; Takenaka Carpentry Tools Museum.
- [medium] Kezurou-kai thin-shaving contests (since the late 1990s), shavings a few microns thick; Kezurou-kai USA since the late 2010s.
- [medium] Nishioka Tsunekazu revived the yari-ganna; Ogawa Mitsuo carries on the lineage.

## Setup
- [high] Most new kanna need setup: fit the blade, open the mouth, flatten the back, sharpen, fit the chipbreaker, true the sole.
- [high] Fit the blade by marking contact points and paring; too tight can split the dai.
- [high] Back flattening (ura-oshi) on a kanaban with kongō-sha, then waterstones.
- [high] Ura-dashi: tap the soft iron on the bevel side over an anvil; never strike the hard steel.
- [medium] Bevel about 25–30°, a single flat bevel, no micro-bevel; corners slightly relieved.
- [high] The chipbreaker must meet the back with no gap; set ≈0.1 mm or closer for fine work.
- [medium] The sole is deliberately not flat: finishing planes touch at the front edge and just ahead of the mouth.
- [high] True the sole with a dai-naoshi with the blade installed but retracted.
- [high] Wooden bodies move with humidity; re-true seasonally; oil pad (abura-tsubo); camellia oil.
- [medium] A worn mouth is repaired by inlaying a block (kuchi-ume).
- [medium] Synthetic stones (Shapton, Naniwa, King, Suehiro); Kyoto natural finishing stones prized; Atoma diamond plates for flattening.

## Debates
- [medium] Chipbreaker vs single blade (the Kato & Kawai study, rediscovered around 2012).
- [medium] Old legendary smiths vs new production; steel-label lore with little controlled testing; natural vs synthetic stones; how much sole relief; whether wooden dai suit dry climates.

- [low] Prices: mass-market $40–150; a good hand-forged blade with dai $200–600; top smiths $1,000+.
