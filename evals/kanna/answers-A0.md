## Q1
I can't confirm Funahiro's current order status. Before you pay for a "new" one, check with a dealer.

What I'm fairly confident of: Funahiro (舟弘) blades come from Funatsu-san's very small workshop in Yoita, which is now part of Nagaoka, Niigata. He is not a young man, and output has been small for years. When something "changes" with a smith in that position, it usually means one of three things:
- His order book has closed.
- He has stopped forging because of age or illness.
- What's being sold is remaining blade stock, fitted to new dai by a dai-maker.

I can't tell you which of these, if any, applies right now. I also can't confirm "Genshin" as a current Funahiro line, so ask exactly what steel and finish you would be getting.

Practical steps:
- Ask a Japanese retailer that has bought from him directly whether new blades are still being forged, or whether they're selling existing stock.
- Search the Japanese second-hand market for 舟弘 寸八 (寸八 is the 70 mm class). Unused old stock and lightly used Funahiro blades do turn up.
- On any used blade, check the remaining length, the ura, and look for cracks at the edge or along the steel/iron line. Budget for re-fitting or replacing the dai.

On who else in Yoita is forging kanna: my understanding is that very few people, if any besides him, still do. Yoita's remaining smiths are better known now for nomi, axes and chōna. I'm not confident naming another Yoita smith who is actively forging plane blades today, and I'd rather not send you after someone who has retired. The local Echigo Yoita uchihamono trade body, or a Niigata tool dealer, can tell you who is actually working. If the answer is "no one," a second-hand Funahiro is your realistic route to a Yoita kanna. Otherwise, widen the search to hand-forging smiths in Miki or Sanjo.
## Q2
Most of these aren't modern steel grades you can look up in a mill catalog. They are trade names for particular stocks of steel. Most are old: imported or early Japanese high-carbon steels, known by a brand or by marks stamped on the bar. Smiths bought them decades ago and are working through what's left. In Japan they're often grouped together as "old steels" (古鋼). Reliable compositions are rarely published, and much of what's written about them is lore.

- **Togo "Ray" (東郷鋼):** an old named steel prized for finishing planes. I'm not certain of its origin or what "Ray" refers to, so treat specific origin stories with caution.
- **Inukubi (犬首, "dog's head"), Tsubame (燕, "swallow") and CWA:** these read like the trademark or stamp names old bar stock was known by in the trade. I can't give you trustworthy origins or analyses. They're generally understood to be plain high-carbon steels valued for purity.
- **Swedish steels (Sandvik, ASSAB/Uddeholm):** very clean plain-carbon steels with low phosphorus and sulfur. They have a long reputation in Japan for planes and saws. I'm not sure exactly what grade "K120" denotes.

What they're supposed to be good at:
- taking a very keen edge easily, especially on natural stones
- a resilient edge (粘り, nebari) that resists micro-chipping
- very clean finishing cuts in softwoods like hinoki and sugi

That's why they appear mostly on finishing planes. They usually aren't claimed to beat Aogami for wear resistance. There is little controlled testing behind any of these claims. The smith's forging and heat treatment probably matter as much as the steel, and much of the price premium is rarity and prestige.

Are any still made?
- **Togo, Inukubi, Tsubame and CWA:** as far as I know, these are old stock only, with no current production. Supply is finite and shrinking. You can't verify the steel yourself, so you are relying on the maker's honesty.
- **Swedish carbon steel is the exception.** Swedish mills still make high-purity carbon strip and bar. Sandvik's materials business is now Alleima, and ASSAB distributes Uddeholm steel. So a "Swedish steel" kanna can be new steel, while "old Swedish steel" (古スウェーデン鋼) is sold separately as vintage stock. Ask which one you're paying for.
## Q3
**First few weeks: let it acclimate and don't adjust anything.**
- **Take the wedging pressure off today.** Tap the blade back, or remove the blade and chipbreaker. A white-oak dai going from Japanese humidity into Arizona air will shrink noticeably. A blade that's tight now can split the dai's side walls or crack the mouth as the wood dries around it. Wipe the blade with camellia oil.
- **Slow the drying.** For the first week or two, keep the plane in a paper bag or closed box in the room where it will live, then leave it out. Keep it away from sun, HVAC vents, swamp coolers and hot cars.
- **Track it by weight.** Weigh the dai on a 0.1 g scale every few days. When the weight stops dropping, it has acclimated; that often takes 3–6 weeks in a desert climate. Watch for checks at the ends.
- **Ignore the "dead flat" sole for now.** It will move as the wood dries, and it changes again once the blade is tensioned. It will also shift between monsoon and dry months.

**Then, in this order:**
1. **Fit the blade.** Rub pencil graphite on the blade's front face and along the edges of the back where the grooves bear. Tap it in, withdraw it, and pare the marked high spots on the bed and in the side grooves (osae-mizo), a little at a time. Aim for even bearing. Under hand pressure the blade should stop a few millimetres short of the mouth, then reach cutting depth with a few moderate taps.
2. **True the sole with the blade in.** Set the blade and chipbreaker at working tension with the edge just retracted, because the wedge pressure bends the dai. Check lengthwise and on both diagonals (for twist) with a trustworthy straightedge. Correct it with a dai-naoshi kanna or a card scraper, not sandpaper, which rounds the sole.
3. **Shape the sole for finishing.** Leave three narrow contact lands: both ends of the sole, plus a strip immediately ahead of the edge (the mouth lip). Relieve the areas between them by only a few hundredths of a millimetre, just enough to see a sliver of light under the straightedge. Keep the sole straight across its width.
4. **Set up the chipbreaker (uragane).** Hone a small, steep bevel on its leading edge and polish the top just behind it. It must bear on the blade's back across the full width, with no light showing. Tap the bent ears so it doesn't rock and is held firmly under the pin. For finish work, set it about 0.1 mm behind the edge and parallel to it. Go closer on tear-out-prone grain, and a bit further back on clean softwood.

Re-check the sole often. On a finishing plane, dai-naoshi (truing the sole) is routine maintenance, not a one-time job.

The #3 grades are Shirogami #3, Kigami #3 (yellow) and the stainless Ginsan (Silver #3). If a listing says "Aogami #3," ask what it actually is. On your reasoning:
- **Carbon content:** the lowest-carbon blue steel is Aogami #2, at about 1.05–1.15% C plus roughly 1–1.5% tungsten and some chromium. Shirogami #1 actually has more carbon (about 1.25–1.35%) but no tungsten or chromium.
- **Ease of sharpening:** this depends more on alloy carbides than on carbon content. The tungsten and chromium carbides in blue steel make it somewhat slower to sharpen. Many people also find it a bit less willing to take the very finest edge on natural stones. White steel is generally the easier of the two to get keen, which is why it's the traditional steel for finishing planes.
- **Edge holding:** blue steel generally goes longer between sharpenings, especially in hard or abrasive woods. In a finishing plane on clean softwood the gap is modest, and the smith's heat treatment matters more than the label.
- **Hardness:** Japanese plane blades in both families usually run around HRC 62–65. Shirogami #1 is often at the top of that range and Aogami #2 is commonly around 62–64. Makers rarely publish exact figures.

For a first finishing kanna, I'd choose Shirogami #1 from a reputable maker. Shirogami #2 is a little more forgiving of chipping and of beginner sharpening. Go with Aogami #2 only if you mostly plane hard or abrasive woods. Most makers' blue-steel kanna are Aogami #2; #1 and Super are less common.

For a spring visit:

- Visit the Miki City Hardware Museum (金物資料館), next to the Kanamono Shrine. It holds periodic traditional forging demonstrations.
- The hardware hall at the Michi-no-Eki Miki roadside station sells local makers' tools, including kanna.

## Q6
Ura-dashi works by stretching the soft iron (jigane) so the edge region bends very slightly toward the back. You then lap the back (ura-oshi) to bring the flat strip along the edge (ito-ura) back.

**What to hit:** the soft-iron part of the bevel (kiriha), close to the line where the hard steel begins but never on the steel. Also avoid the edge itself and the corners. Start in the middle of the width and work outward evenly, staying a few millimetres in from each side.

**What to hit it with:** a small hammer of roughly 100–200 g with a slightly domed face, or a dedicated ura-dashi hammer with a narrow rounded peen. Don't use your regular gennō swung hard.

**What to rest it on:** a small anvil or a solid hardened steel block on a firm bench, with the blade back-down. The critical point is that the back must be supported directly under each blow. Because of the hollow (ura-suki), the spot under the blow may not be touching a flat anvil. Tilt the blade, or use the anvil's edge, so the back bears right there. Hammering an unsupported blade is how blades crack. A ringing note usually means the blade is unsupported; a dull "tok" means it's bearing.

**Technique:**
- In cold weather, warm the blade first until it feels comfortably warm. Cold hard steel is more brittle.
- Use light, controlled taps in rows across the width, not a few heavy blows.
- After every few taps, rub the back briefly on a flat kanaban or diamond plate and see where the bright strip appears. Stop once it runs the full width at the edge.
- Then do a proper ura-oshi on a flat plate with carborundum, or on a coarse diamond plate. Use a stick (ura-oshi bō) to keep pressure near the edge without rounding it, then finish on your stones.
- Doing a little often is better than waiting until the ura-gire is severe.

**The HSS kanna:** no, don't peen it. High-speed steel tolerates hammering far less well. Many HSS blades also aren't the traditional forge-welded laminate of steel on soft iron that ura-dashi relies on. Most makers say not to tap them out, but check what yours says. Instead, restore the flat by lapping the back on a coarse diamond plate. The hollow will shrink over time, so lap the back more often. When the hollow is nearly gone, the maker or a professional sharpener can re-grind it.
## Q7
Hitachi itself no longer makes them, but the same steels are still made at the same plant.

**What changed:** Hitachi sold its stake in Hitachi Metals to a group of investors led by Bain Capital. In January 2023 the company was renamed Proterial (プロテリアル), and it is no longer part of the Hitachi group. Shirogami and Aogami are still produced at the Yasugi Works in Shimane, under the YSS (Yasugi Specialty Steel) line: Shirogami #1/#2/#3, Aogami #1/#2/Super and so on. The change was in ownership and naming, not metallurgy. People still say "Hitachi White #2" out of habit.

**Are they going away?** I haven't seen any official notice discontinuing Shirogami or Aogami for edge tools, though I'm not certain nothing has changed recently. The visible effects have been higher prices and occasional shortages of particular sizes or of clad stock from distributors. The real long-term threat to kanna in these steels is the dwindling number of smiths, not the steel itself.

**"Aigami":** I'm not aware of any Proterial grade by that name. It could be a maker's or retailer's own name, or a mix-up with Aogami or with Shiroko/Aoko, the alternative names some makers use. It could also be something recent that I can't confirm. Before paying a premium, I'd want a published composition and a track record in plane blades.

**Should you look for a kanna in Aigami?** No. Choose the maker first and the steel second. Shirogami and Aogami remain the proven, widely available steels for plane blades, and nothing about the ownership change is a reason to switch.
## Q8
As far as I know, yes. Kezurou-kai (削ろう会) resumed its national meets after the pandemic pause and has kept going. I can't confirm the date or venue of the next national meet, though. The venue moves around Japan, and each meet is announced on the association's Japanese website and social media, usually months ahead. Check there rather than relying on forum posts. Overseas spin-offs such as Kezurou-kai USA run on their own schedules.

The spread from 2 to 20 microns mostly comes from comparing different things:
- **Elite results at the national meets:** continuous, full-width hinoki shavings measured with a micrometer. In recent years the winning shavings have generally been reported at roughly 3–6 µm.
- **Claims of 2 µm or less:** these exist, but a standard micrometer can barely resolve that on a shaving that compresses. Treat them as the single thinnest reading, not a typical shaving.
- **Around 10 µm:** an excellent result for a strong amateur, and typical of many overseas event winners.
- **15–20 µm:** good work by most standards. Figures like these often come from harder or less ideal wood, early rounds, or casual demonstrations.

The numbers also depend on how the shaving was made and measured:
- **Wood and preparation:** clear, straight-grained, moisture-conditioned hinoki is far easier than anything else.
- **Humidity.**
- **What the figure means:** the thinnest point or an average along the shaving.
- **The micrometer:** shavings compress, so the instrument and how hard its jaws close matter.
- **The rules:** whether the shaving had to be unbroken over the full length and width.

I'm not certain of the most recent winning figures, so take 3–6 µm as the recent elite range rather than a precise record.
