Yoita itself has shrunk. Three of the guild's five member forges are closed: Funahiro, Nakano chisels (中野鑿) and Usui Hamono (碓氷刃物). The Yoita knife-craft hall shut in February 2025 ([Yoita guild](https://yoita-uchihamono.com/master/), [Niigata Nippo](https://www.niigata-nippo.co.jp/articles/-/562988)). These are the smiths still forging there:

- **Mizuno Seisuke (水野清介)** is about 70 and was a pupil of Usui Kengo. His marks include 和楽 (Waraku) and 倭心. For a Yoita 寸八 smoother in the Funahiro class, he is the first maker to look at.
- **Mizudori Hamono (水鳥刃物)** is Nitori Tōru (似鳥透, born 1989, a former Nikon engineer). He apprenticed with Mizuno from 2020 and has worked on his own since October 2025. He makes two lines: 朱雀 Suzaku, with a wrought-iron backing, and 羽根印, with a mild-steel backing ([Mizudori](https://mizudori-hamono.com/about/profile/)). He has only a year's record on his own, but he carries the Usui–Mizuno line forward.
- **The Komori brothers (小森)** make only small planes (小鉋). They are Japan's only small-plane specialists and have no successor. They won't make your smoother, but buy from them while you can.

I don't have current prices or waiting times for Mizuno or Mizudori. Contact them in Japanese, or go through a Japanese dealer that sells bare blades (Kurashige, Mandaraya). If you'll look outside Yoita, Chiyozuru Sadahide in Miki is in the same price tier.

## Q2
I can place some of these steels and not others. For none of them is there evidence that they plane better than Shirogami or Aogami.

**Swedish steels (スウェーデン鋼).** In Japanese catalogs this means imported plain high-carbon steel. The reputation goes back to the purity of Swedish iron (low in phosphorus and sulphur), which Sheffield's crucible-steel makers relied on. Japanese smiths have long prized imported Western steel. Makers sell it as clean and fine-grained, easy to sharpen to a keen edge, and "tenacious" (粘りがある) rather than chippy.

Sandvik's steel business has been Alleima since 2022 and still makes strip steel for knives and razors. ASSAB is the sales arm of Uddeholm, part of voestalpine. I can't confirm what "K120" is chemically, or whether either company still rolls the grade in a given kanna. Some of what's being forged is surely bar stock bought years ago. Swedish steel isn't only a vintage story, though: Tsunesaburō launched a new Swedish-steel model, 汐音, in November 2025 ([Tsunesaburō](https://www.tsune36.co.jp/)).

**Togo "Ray", Inukubi, Tsubame, CWA.** I can't reliably identify these. I don't know the mill, the composition or whether they're still made, and I won't guess.

**How to judge any of them:**
- Ask the seller three things: which mill and grade, whether it's current production or remaining stock, and what hardness. If they can't answer, the premium is paying for a story.
- No controlled wood-planing test compares even Shirogami, Aogami and HSS. The one rigorous test of a "legendary" steel was Knife Steel Nerds on tamahagane, which found it no better than ordinary low-alloy steel such as 26C3 ([KSN](https://knifesteelnerds.com/2025/09/10/testing-tamahagane-traditional-japanese-steel/)). Expect a plain carbon steel to behave like one. The smith's forge-welding and heat treatment matter more, and so does the dai.
- Some old stock is real. Jigane is mostly salvaged wrought iron from bridges, anchor chain and temple nails, and gokunantetsu reportedly isn't made any more. But scarcity also sells, so treat lines like "very similar to tamahagane" as marketing.
- If you want an unusual steel that's definitely in current production, try Proterial's HAP40 powder HSS, as in Tsunesaburō's flat-backed "Miki" plane. Its longevity claims are untested too.

## Q3
**Weeks 1–3: let the dai settle before you adjust anything.**
- Take the blade and chipbreaker out now. Tap the end of the dai that the blade leans toward, alternating corners; don't pry. A dai moved from humid Japan into dry air can split, and the main risk is a tight blade in a shrinking body. The usual advice is to wait about a week; in Arizona I'd allow two to three.
- Slow the drying at first by keeping the dai in its box or loosely wrapped. Keep it out of the sun and away from heaters and AC vents, and watch the mouth and grooves for checks.
- Ignore the "dead flat" sole for now. It will move as the oak dries, and you have to judge it with the blade at working tension anyway.
- Sharpen in the meantime. Flatten the back, keeping a narrow flat at the edge (ito-ura) of 0.5–1 mm. Hone a single flat bevel of about 28–30°, or 33–35° for hardwoods, and round the corners slightly.

**Then fit the blade.** New planes ship tight on purpose, "90% finished", and the final fitting (仕込み) is the owner's job. Mark the blade with pencil or oil, tap it in and pull it out, then pare the marked high spots in the bed and grooves. Repeat until the blade seats in about 3–4 firm mallet blows with about 1 mm of side play. Avoid hard contact at the bed's front corners, because they split. Exactly where the blade should grip is contested: Japanese dealers want full bed contact with a faint gap at the sides, while Covington wants pressure only in the strips beside the grooves ([Covington](https://covingtonandsons.com/2021/05/08/japanese-handplanes-part-4-fitting-blade-body/), [Fukada](https://daiku-manual.com/ken/2020/04/13/kanna2/)).

**Chipbreaker.** There must be no gap between it and the blade's back; check against the light. For finishing, set it "one hair" back, about 0.1–0.3 mm, and only as close as you need to stop tearout. Too close jams the plane and dulls the finish, and Covington's 0.5–0.8 mm is an outlier. Keep its secondary bevel narrow, because a wide one jams shavings. Mandaraya grinds it at about 51° to the blade; Covington uses 70–80°. Fix a skewed chipbreaker at the cross-pin (押え棒), not by bending its ears. Aim for a mouth of about 0.3 mm ([Mandaraya](https://daiku-dougu.jp/kannna-osaebou.html), [mouth](https://daiku-dougu.jp/2a-2.htm)).

**Do the sole last, with the blade in at working tension** and the edge just retracted, because blade pressure bulges the sole behind the mouth. Relieve everything except the bearing bands by a paper's thickness or less (Covington says 0.2–0.5 mm). If a clear band of light shows under the straightedge, you've taken off too much. Which bands to keep is contested. Tsunesaburō says a finishing plane bears only at the mouth and at the 台尻, which, confusingly, is the end that leads as you pull. Kakuri's diagrams show the opposite end ([Tsunesaburō](https://www.tsune36.co.jp/kanna_kaitai_shinsho/page_003/), [Kakuri](https://www.kakuri.co.jp/)). Either way, the strip just in front of the mouth must bear. After that, don't re-true on a schedule. Each truing opens the mouth, so only touch the burnished spots, and only when the plane misbehaves.

## Q4
Not quite. There are two problems with the reasoning.

**As far as I know, there is no Aogami #3.** The blue-paper family from Hitachi, now Proterial, is Aogami #1, #2 and Super. The only #3 is in white paper (Shirogami #3). Proterial's current catalog is behind a request form, so I can't confirm this today, but I wouldn't expect to find a #3 there. That means I can't give you a hardness or a list of makers for it. If a listing says "Aogami #3", ask the seller what steel it really is: a typo for #2, a mislabelled Shirogami #3, or a house name.

**White and blue aren't steps on a carbon ladder.** In the Hitachi-era catalog, Shirogami #1 has about 1.25–1.35% carbon, the same as Aogami #1 and more than Aogami #2 (about 1.05–1.15%). Blue paper is the same clean base with chromium and tungsten added ([catalog](https://knifesteelnerds.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/03/Hitachi-cutlery-steel.pdf)). So the lowest-carbon blue steel has *less* carbon than the Shirogami #1 you're weighing it against.

**What's known about performance.** The usual lore is that white steel sharpens more easily to a keener edge and blue lasts a little longer. That's plausible, but nobody has run a controlled planing test. Proterial recommends both for planes: Aogami for the "finest planes", and Shirogami #1 for the best planes and chisels. In knife tests, Aogami Super was less tough than 26C3, a close analogue of Shirogami #1 ([KSN](https://knifesteelnerds.com/)). The smith sets the hardness, not the label. Few makers publish it, but kanna in either steel are generally hardened to the low-to-mid 60s HRC.

**What I'd do:** choose the maker and the dai first. Then take Shirogami #1 or Aogami #2 (or #1), whichever that maker does best. For a first finishing plane, Shirogami #1 is a sound, forgiving steel to learn sharpening on. A typical mid-range example is Hida's "Suika" by Tsunesaburō, in Aogami, at $410.90 ([Hida](https://hidatool.com/list/c/hira-smoothing-planes)).

## Q6
**What you hit:** only the soft iron (jigane) on the bevel. Never hit the hard steel or the back. On the bevel, the hard steel shows as a bright band next to the edge, with the iron above it. Start tapping on the iron about 2–3 mm behind that steel line. Concentrate where the flat has disappeared, which is usually the middle first, and keep it even across the width.

**On what:** an anvil or steel block with a slightly rounded corner. Lay the blade back down, with the rounded corner directly under the spot you're striking. Peening the iron stretches the bevel side and bends the edge very slightly toward the back, so honing brings the flat back to the edge. Listen as you work. A dull "kotsu-kotsu" means the blade is properly supported. A ringing note means it's bridging over air, which is how blades crack, so stop and reposition it.

**With what:** a small, light hammer with a slightly rounded face. Give it many light taps rather than a few hard ones. In a cold shop, warm the blade to hand temperature first.

**Then:** after every few taps, rub the back on a flat stone or diamond plate. Stop as soon as the flat reaches the edge again; over-tapping just leaves a hump to grind off. Re-establish a narrow flat at the edge (ito-ura) of about 0.5–1 mm. A 3–4 mm strip makes the chipbreaker easier to fit. Lapping on a kanaban with loose grit is still taught, but working professionals now treat it as optional. Flat stones or diamond plates give more consistent results.

**Your HSS blade: no.** HSS kanna, such as Tsunesaburō's HSS and HAP40 blades, have a flat back with no hollow and are sold as needing no ura-dashi ([Tsunesaburō](https://www.tsune36.co.jp/)). When the back wears, just lap it flat, preferably on diamond plates, which cut HSS's hard carbides far better than conventional stones. Don't take a hammer to it. There's no hollow to restore, and you'd be striking a very hard, brittle steel. If yours turns out to have a soft-iron backing and a hollow, ask its maker before you try anything.

## Q7
**The owner and the name changed, not the steels.** Hitachi sold Hitachi Metals to a group led by Bain Capital, and on 4 January 2023 the company became Proterial. Since 1 July 2026, Bain's holding company itself has traded as Proterial, Ltd. The YSS (Yasugi Specialty Steel) line, which includes the paper steels, sits in its Aero & Industrial Alloy unit. The company aims to list on the stock market again ([Proterial](https://www.proterial.com/press/2026/)). The "paper" names (white, yellow, blue) still refer to the coloured labels on the bars.

**Is white or blue going away?** There's no official sign. Proterial still lists 白紙 (Shirogami), 黄紙 (Kigami) and 青紙 (Aogami), and it hasn't published any discontinuation notice. You'll hear stories of a 250% price rise and of Shirogami #3 and Kigami being dropped. Those come from dealers and forums, not from Proterial, and a scarcity story also helps sell stock. Even the rumours are about Shirogami #3 and Kigami, not the #1 and #2 grades that most good kanna use. The current catalog is behind a request form, so it's unknown, as of today, whether prices or the grade list have quietly changed.

**Aigami (藍紙)** is a new grade, announced on 9 December 2025 and described as "neither carbon nor stainless". Aigami No. 2 is in mass production, and it's aimed at knives and razors, not planes ([Proterial](https://www.proterial.com/press/2025/n1209.html)). I don't know of any kanna maker using it, there's no planing data, and I can't give you its composition. So no, I wouldn't go looking for an Aigami kanna. A Shirogami or Aogami blade from a good smith is still the sensible choice, and the smith's work and the dai matter far more than the grade.

**The numbers.** Both ends of your range are real, but they come from different rounds:
- **Preliminaries**, on the competitors' own hinoki boards: winners take about 3–4 µm, and 2 µm shavings were already being pinned up in 2009.
- **Finals**, on boards the organisers supply: much thicker, about 15–50 µm.
- There's no official record list, and claims of 1 µm are unverified.

Under the 2025 rules, you bring your own hinoki board (at least 55 × 1,800 mm), your plane must be under 76 mm, and each shaving is measured at three points ([Katano rules](https://kezuroukai-katano.to-ryou.com/competition/)).

Don't compare these numbers with shop work. Competitors:
- bring pre-sharpened planes
- vacuum-seal their tools and boards, and keep the boards damp
- favour single-blade dai with tight mouths

Judges allow only 15 seconds to spread a shaving before it's measured. Going under 10 µm takes control "far from the reality of day-to-day work" ([Paklan](https://www.paklan.org/a-few-frames-from-kezuroukai-japan-2024/)).
