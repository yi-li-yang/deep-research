## Q2
These are mostly legacy or rare steels. The higher prices reflect scarcity and difficult forging more than a proven edge over a well-made Aogami or Shirogami blade.

- **Togo steel (東郷鋼), "Ray" grade (東郷レイ号).**
  - In the late Meiji era, the Tokyo importer Kawai Yōkō Shōten (河合洋鋼商店) had British and Swedish mills make high-carbon, high-tungsten alloy steels. They were first meant for scalpels and metal-cutting tools, and were named after Admiral Tōgō. Japanese sources usually credit a British firm, "Andrew(s)", as the maker.
  - Ray is the grade used for planes. It is hard to forge-weld and a little harder to sharpen, but the edge lasts a very long time ([Yamamoto Kanna steel list](https://yamamotokanna.sakura.ne.jp/siyoukousyu.html), [Japan-Tool](https://www.japan-tool.com/tech_knlg/Togo_Reigou_and_Yokoyama_Kunio.html)).
  - Imports ended by WWII, so only finite old stock remains ([research blog](https://ameblo.jp/seisyutanukikoubou/entry-12920686887.html)).
- **Inukubi (犬首, "dog's-head" stamp).**
  - A related steel from the same Kawai line. Yamamoto says it is close to Togo Ray but even harder to forge and to harden. It doesn't feel hard on the stone and lasts well.
  - Sources disagree on what it is: Tsunesaburo calls its composition HSS-like ([Tsunesaburo](https://www.tsune36.co.jp/kanna_kaitai_shinsho/page_001/)), while Japan-Tool says it is nearer to white steel.
  - Old stock only.
- **CWA (安来CWA).** A Yasugi (Hitachi) tungsten tool steel, roughly equivalent to the old JIS SKS1, at about 1.39% C and 4.65% W. It is a hardwood steel and no longer made (Yamamoto list above).
- **Tsubame (燕/ツバメ鋼).**
  - Made about 40 years ago. Yamamoto says even its analysis is uncertain: modest carbon but plenty of tungsten.
  - It is tough and resists chipping, even on sugi end grain, and is aimed at medium to hard woods ([Honmamon listing](https://www.honmamon.jp/c/gr14/gr33)).
  - I know of no current production, so treat it as stock steel.
- **Swedish steels (Sandvik, ASSAB K120).**
  - Very clean, plain high-carbon steels. K120 is roughly 1.0–1.25% C with a trace of Cr.
  - They behave much like Shirogami #1: easy to sharpen, very keen, with a glossy finish. They do best on softwoods and medium woods ([Tsunesaburo](https://www.tsune36.co.jp/kanna_kaitai_shinsho/page_001/)).
  - Swedish carbon steel is still made. The bars smiths prize, though, are often old stock, such as K120 at "old stock 1.2% carbon" ([Brian Holcombe](https://brianholcombewoodworker.com/guide-to-japanese-chisels/)). Treat "charcoal-smelted, almost tamahagane" sales copy as lore.

"Mizuno" is the Yoita smith in Q1. He uses Togo, Swedish and K120 steel alongside Shirogami #1 and Aogami #1. For scale, Honmamon lists a 70 mm Togo Ray plane at ¥138,000 and a Tsubame one at ¥46,500.

## Q3
Expect the dai to move. White oak that left Japan at ordinary humidity will shrink in Arizona air, and a blade that is already tight is exactly what splits the cheeks of a drying dai.

**First 2–4 weeks**
- Take the blade out, or at least tap it well back. For planes moving from humid Japan to a dry climate, Covington advises removing the blade and letting the body acclimatize for a week or so ([Covington](https://covingtonandsons.com/2021/05/08/japanese-handplanes-part-4-fitting-blade-body/)). In Arizona I'd allow longer. Brian Holcombe lets a new dai sit for a few weeks and sets it up only once it stops moving ([Holcombe](https://brianholcombewoodworker.com/2016/08/11/kanna-dai-prep/)).
- Keep the plane in the shop, away from sun, heaters, cooler or AC drafts, and hot cars. Check the sole weekly with a straightedge and winding sticks.
- Use the waiting time on the blade: lap the back, sharpen it, and fit the chipbreaker.
- Once the dai stops changing, refit the blade. A good fit goes in with about three medium mallet blows and adjusts in three or four taps (Covington). If it's still too tight, mark the contact spots with pencil and pare the grooves a hair at a time. Pare sparingly, because monsoon humidity will swell the dai again.
- From then on, store the plane with the blade in place but retracted just above the sole ([Tsunesaburo](https://www.tsune36.co.jp/kanna_kaitai_shinsho/page_005/)).

**Sole for a finish plane**
- Always work the sole with the blade and chipbreaker installed, set just shy of cutting. Blade pressure pushes the sole out around the mouth ([Kakuri](https://www.kakuri.co.jp/2017/01/25/%E9%89%8B%E3%81%AE%E3%81%8A%E6%89%8B%E5%85%A5%E3%82%8C/)).
- Leave two narrow bearing lands: one at the front and one just ahead of the mouth. Relieve everything else, especially behind the mouth.
- A finisher needs only a hair of relief, enough for a thin line of light under a straightedge ([Giant Cypress](https://giantcypress.net/tagged/Japanese%20plane%20setup)). Coarser planes get up to about 0.2 mm ([example](https://hajityoro.com/mokkou10-kannadai-sitaba)).
- Some people prefer the mouth plus the heel instead (Holcombe). Pick one layout and stay consistent.
- Cut the relief with a dai-naoshi scraper plane or a card scraper, and recheck the sole before each session.

**Chipbreaker**
- Its edge should seat on the blade with no light showing across the full width, with a narrow, steep micro-bevel.
- Tap or bend the ears until it needs a few firm taps to seat and presses at the edge instead of rocking on the ears (Kakuri).
- For finishing, start about 0.1–0.2 mm behind the edge. Move it closer only until tear-out stops; set too close, it adds resistance and clogs.

## Q4
Your reasoning has a snag: there is no Aogami #3. Proterial (formerly Hitachi Metals) makes Shirogami 1, 2 and 3, Kigami 2, and Aogami 1, 2 and Super, and nothing else in that family ([Proterial YSS](https://www.proterial.com/e/yss/search/aogami_super.html)). A few knife-marketing pages mention "Aogami No. 3", but it is not a mill grade. If a seller lists it, ask what the steel really is; it's probably Aogami #2 or Shirogami #3.

The lowest-carbon blue is Aogami #2: about 1.05–1.15% C, 1–1.5% W and 0.2–0.5% Cr ([composition summary](https://www.knivesandstones.com.au/pages/white-vs-blue-steel)). That is already less carbon than Shirogami #1 (1.25–1.35% C), which shows why your logic doesn't hold. How easily a steel sharpens depends mainly on the tungsten and chromium carbides, not the carbon number.
- Any Aogami usually feels slower on the stones than Shirogami #1.
- Shirogami is the steel known for sharpening easily, biting well and leaving a glossy surface. Aogami is valued for edge life and as an all-rounder ([Tsunesaburo](https://www.tsune36.co.jp/kanna_kaitai_shinsho/page_001/)).
- Among the blues, #2 is somewhat easier to sharpen and tougher than #1.

**Hardness:** Proterial only specifies 60+ HRC. Kanna smiths generally harden into the low-to-mid 60s, but few publish figures, and the smith's forging and heat treatment matter more than the label.

**Makers:** Aogami #2 kanna are fairly uncommon. Keizaburo in Miki makes them; Big Sand Woodworking ordered a 70 mm blue #2 blade from him ([Big Sand](https://www.bigsandwoodworking.com/blacksmith-visit-keizaburo/)). Most makers use Aogami #1 or Super (Tsunesaburo, Mizuno). Shirogami #1 is easy to find, for example Mizuno's Shisei.

For a first finishing plane I'd choose Shirogami #1 from a good smith. It's easier to learn sharpening on, and it gives the best surface on softwoods.

## Q6
Ura-dashi works by stretching the soft iron so the hard steel bows toward the back. Success depends on two things: never touching the steel, and always backing up each blow.

- **Warm the blade first** when it's cool, to body temperature or in warm water. Cold steel cracks more easily ([daiku-dougu](https://daiku-dougu.jp/kannna-tatakikata.html)).
- **What to hit:** only the soft iron (jigane) on the bevel side.
  - Covington marks a band that starts 2–3 mm back from the steel/iron line and runs to the end of the iron.
  - About two-thirds of the blows go in the middle third of the width, staying about 3 mm off the sides ([Covington](https://covingtonandsons.com/2021/04/24/sharpening-japanese-tools-part-30-uradashi-uraoshi/)).
  - Kakuri gives the same rule: tap the middle of the soft iron, and expect the blade to crack if you strike the hard steel at the edge ([Kakuri](https://www.kakuri.co.jp/2017/01/25/%E9%89%8B%E3%81%AE%E3%81%8A%E6%89%8B%E5%85%A5%E3%82%8C/)).
- **With what:** a small, light hammer with a small face. Options are a ~15 mm hazuchi (刃槌) or pointed hammer (Kakuri), the pointed end of a small genno, or the peen of a Warrington or tack hammer (Covington). Use many precise light taps, not a few heavy ones ([JTAS](https://www.japanesetools.com.au/blogs/jtas-plane-tips/tuning-japanese-planes-addendum-b-ura-dashi-tapping)).
- **On what:** a heavy steel anvil with a rounded corner or crowned face, such as a rail section, a kanatoko or a 目振り台.
  - Lay the blade back-down with the anvil directly under the spot you strike.
  - A good blow sounds solid, not rattly. Unsupported blows are what crack blades.
- **Check often:** sight a straightedge against the light, or take a few strokes on the kanaban. Stop once the edge zone stands proud.
- **Then ura-oshi:**
  - Use a kanaban with a little carborundum. Kakuri suggests about half a teaspoon and three drops of water.
  - Keep the pressure near the edge, so you restore a thin land and keep the hollow.
  - Then move to fine stones.

**HSS:** generally, don't tap it out. Many HSS kanna are built so they never need it. Tsunesaburo sells its SKH51 and HAP40 lines as ura-dashi-free, with no side lands (a gentle curve toward the edge) or no hollow at all. On those you simply hone the back again; a diamond plate makes that much faster ([SKH51](https://www.tsune36.co.jp/items-service/items003/koganna_skh51/), [HAP40](https://ad-tools.com.au/products/tsunesaburo-hap40-hss-kanna)). If yours has a conventional hollow back, ask the maker before hammering, because HSS is far less forgiving than carbon steel.

## Q7
The paper steels still come from the same Yasugi works, under a new owner and name. There is no public sign that Shirogami or Aogami is being dropped.

- **What changed:** Hitachi sold Hitachi Metals to a Bain Capital-led consortium, and it was renamed Proterial, Ltd. on 4 January 2023 ([timeline](https://note.com/magic_impala5319/n/nbdb1c5bfe41f)).
  - The Yasugi works and the YSS / Yasuki Hagane brands continued.
  - Proterial's catalog still lists Shirogami 1/2/3, Kigami 2 and Aogami 1/2/Super. Aogami Super's listed uses include "High quality Plane" ([Proterial YSS](https://www.proterial.com/e/yss/search/aogami_super.html)).
  - The 2026 reorganization split magnets and cable/auto parts off into subsidiaries, while specialty steels stayed in the parent company ([Japan Metal Daily](https://www.japanmetaldaily.com/articles/-/251085)).
- **Aigami (藍紙®):** launched on 9 December 2025 as a "new category" between carbon and stainless steel ([Proterial release](https://www.proterial.com/press/2025/n1209.html)).
  - 藍紙2号 is in mass production. It claims hardness equal to or above Aogami #2, with corrosion resistance like GIN3.
  - 藍紙1号 is still in prototype evaluation. It claims hardness above SUS440C, with corrosion resistance like SUS420J2.
  - Both are said to sharpen easily. The target uses are high-end kitchen knives, outdoor knives, razor blades and scalpels; neither planes nor forge-welding are mentioned.
- **Are white and blue going away?** There's no discontinuation notice, and Aigami is an addition to the range. Nobody outside Proterial can see its long-term production plans, so read this as "no signal" rather than a guarantee. The bigger threat to kanna is the dwindling number of smiths, not the steel.
- **Should you look for an Aigami kanna?** Not yet. None appear to be offered, and there are three more reasons to wait:
  - Rust resistance matters little in a plane.
  - Chromium-bearing steels are harder to forge-weld onto soft iron.
  - The steel has no track record in plane blades.
  
  Let the smiths experiment first. A well-made Shirogami or Aogami kanna is still the safe buy.

## Q8
Yes, it's thriving. The 41st national meet was in Katano, Osaka, on 12–13 April 2025 ([Katano Times](https://www.katano-times.com/interview/kezuroukai-katano-20250410.html)). The 42nd was in Miki on 13–14 June 2026, with about 500 participants ([Sun TV](https://www.sun-tv.co.jp/suntvnews/news/2026/07/06/95490/)). **The next is the 43rd: 21–22 November 2026 at Yōkaichiba Dome (八日市場ドーム), Sōsa, Chiba**, with about 400 competitors expected ([press release](https://www.atpress.ne.jp/news/6487820)). I don't know of a confirmed date or venue for the 44th yet.

**What the best actually get:** elite competitors in the general division, using 70 mm (寸八) planes on hinoki, reach about 3–5 µm per measuring point. The 2026 Miki winner reported 3.5 µm in the preliminaries ([Takumi](https://takumi-123.com/news/20260625/)), and Sun TV says top competitors get under 5 µm at a point.

**Why the quoted numbers range from 2 to nearly 20 µm:**
- **2 µm is a record, not a norm.** Idei Hiroyuki set it at the 27th meet. At the 2014 meet he got 5 µm and didn't place ([R-bay report](https://www.rbayakyu.jp/rbay-kodawari/item/1625-30)). The figure is still used in promotion ([atpress](https://www.atpress.ne.jp/news/6487820)).
- **Per point vs. total.** Each shaving is measured at three points, and some reports quote the sum. A total of ≤30 µm (about 10 µm per point) is presented as a respectable target (Sun TV).
- **Different divisions.**
  - The 5-sun plane division (about 150 mm blade) was won with 19 µm in 2024 ([PR Times](https://prtimes.jp/main/html/rd/p/000000013.000085359.html)).
  - Women and students may use planes as small as 寸六 and shorter boards.
- **Preliminaries vs. final.**
  - In the preliminaries, competitors bring their own hinoki (at least 55 mm × 1.8 m) and get many attempts.
  - Finals use the organizers' wood under a time limit, for example 65 mm × 3 m hinoki at Hadano ([rules](https://omotan-hadano.jp/kezuroukai/the40th/competition.html)) or sugi at Kurume in 2018 ([video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=02dRX8l9s3U)). Final numbers therefore run higher.
- **Measurement noise.** At these thicknesses, exactly where and how the micrometer closes on a fibrous shaving matters, so don't over-read 1 µm differences.

For a baseline, a reporter who competed in 2014 managed 15–17 µm, which is roughly where a competent amateur lands.
