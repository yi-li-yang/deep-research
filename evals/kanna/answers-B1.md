## Q2
These are mostly old specialty steels, sold on the promise of long edge life (永切れ). Most are finite stock.

- **Tōgō steel (東郷鋼).**
  - Origin: imported from 1906 by Kawai Yōkō Shōten in Tokyo. It was made chiefly by Andrew & Co. of Sheffield, plus Swedish and German mills, and named, with permission, after Admiral Tōgō ([history](http://ohmura-study.net/006.html)).
  - The name: "Ray" is レイ (rei, "zero"). Yamamoto calls his remnant bars 東郷0号, No. 0 ([Yamamoto](https://yamamotokanna.sakura.ne.jp/gentei.html)). One researcher matches Rei-gō to the old SKS1 tungsten tool-steel class ([blog](https://ameblo.jp/seisyutanukikoubou/entry-12920686887.html)).
  - Supply ended with WWII; today's Tōgō planes are forged from rediscovered bars.
  - Reputation: stays sharp even in sugi, but hard to forge and slow to sharpen.
- **Inukubi (犬首, "dog's head" mark).** A related pre-war steel. Yamamoto says it is like Tōgō Rei but harder still to work. Tsunesaburō calls it near-HSS, very durable, slow to set up, and a collectors' piece.
- **Tsubame (燕).** Made about 40 years ago. No analysis exists; it is reputed to be modest in carbon and high in tungsten. It is a hardwood steel, tough enough not to chip on cedar end grain, and little remains ([Yamamoto steels](https://yamamotokanna.sakura.ne.jp/sp/siyoukousyu.html), [Tsunesaburō](https://www.tsune36.co.jp/kanna_kaitai_shinsho/page_001/)).
- **CWA.** A Hitachi Yasugi tungsten tool steel. Yamamoto gives C 1.39% and W 4.65%, the SKS1 type. Hitachi wrote about it in 1964 ([Hitachi Hyōron](https://www.hitachihyoron.com/jp/pdf/1964/12/1964_12_16.pdf)). It is missing from the 2017 Japanese tool-steel brand table ([JSSA](https://www.tokushuko.or.jp/publication/magazine/toolsteelbrand08.pdf)), so assume old stock.
- **Swedish.** Plain, very clean high-carbon steels, low in phosphorus and sulfur:
  - Sandvik, now [Alleima](https://www.alleima.com/en/technical-center/material-datasheets/strip-steel/alleima-20c/), which still makes 1% C strip.
  - ASSAB K120: about 1–1.2% C with a trace of chromium.

  They behave like Shirogami #1: quick to sharpen, keen, and glossy in softwood ([Mandaraya](https://daiku-dougu.jp/kannna-kona-gahoujin.html)). They're still produced, though a smith's bar may be decades old.

Typical 70 mm prices:

| Steel | Price |
|---|---|
| Tōgō Rei | ¥138,000 |
| Aogami #1 (same dealer) | ¥17,500–50,300 |
| Tsubame | ¥46,500 |
| Swedish (Tsunesaburō Shion) | ¥71,340 |

The first three are from [Honmamon](https://www.honmamon.jp/c/gr14/gr33).

None of the performance claims has been tested in a plane. The premium mostly buys scarcity and difficult forging.

## Q3
Arizona is the risky case. Wood indoors in the dry Southwest settles near 6% moisture ([Wood Handbook](http://www.woodbodger.com/wp-content/uploads/2012/02/Wood-Handbook-Chapter-13-Drying-and-Control-of-Moisture-Content.pdf)), far below oak air-dried in humid Japan. The dai will shrink around a steel blade that doesn't. A body shipped to a desert can crack this way ([Covington](https://covingtonandsons.com/2021/05/08/japanese-handplanes-part-4-fitting-blade-body/)).

**First weeks: change nothing.**
1. Tap out the blade and chipbreaker now, with light, alternating blows on the corners of the 台頭 (back end).
2. Leave the body, blade out, in the room where it will live, out of sun and away from vents.
   - Covington says about a week; in Arizona, give it 2–4.
   - Keep it in its paulownia box for the first few days to slow the drying.
   - Weigh it every few days. When the weight stops falling, it has acclimatized.
3. Meanwhile, flatten the back and sharpen the blade. Watch the sole move with a straightedge, but don't plane it. Its "dead flat" is only the maker's 90% stage.

**Then set it up:**
- **Blade.** Pencil the blade, tap it in, and pare only the burnished spots on the bed and grooves, until 3–4 firm mallet blows seat it. Leave slight side play. If you fit it in the dry season, the monsoon will loosen it rather than tighten it.
- **Sole.** Work with the blade and chipbreaker in at working tension and the edge just retracted. True the sole, then relieve it.
  - For a finisher, Tsunesaburō leaves two bands: the mouth (about 1.5 mm) and the near end (台尻, about 3 mm). The rest is relieved by no more than a sheet of paper ([Tsunesaburō](https://www.tsune36.co.jp/kanna_kaitai_shinsho/page_003/)).
  - Some carpenters keep both ends plus the mouth ([Fukada](https://daiku-manual.com/ken/2020/04/13/kanna2/)).
  - After that, re-true only when the plane misbehaves.
- **Chipbreaker.**
  - No light should show where it meets the blade's back.
  - Tune its ears so it springs firmly against the pin.
  - Set it about a hair (0.1–0.2 mm) behind the edge, and closer only for tearout.
  - Keep the mouth at about 0.3 mm ([Mandaraya](https://daiku-dougu.jp/2a-2.htm)).

Store the plane with the blade backed off slightly.

## Q4
First, there's no Aogami #3. Proterial's Yasuki range has Aogami No. 1, No. 2 and Super, plus Shirogami 1–3 and Kigami 2 ([Proterial](https://www.proterial.com/e/yss/search/aogami2.html)). A listing that says "Aogami #3" is mislabelled, probably meaning #2 or a white or yellow #3, so ask the seller.

The lowest-carbon blue is Aogami #2, and it reverses your premise: it has less carbon than Shirogami #1 ([Aogami 2](https://www.proterial.com/e/yss/search/aogami2.html), [Shirogami 1](https://www.proterial.com/e/yss/search/shirogami1.html)).

| Steel | Carbon | Alloy |
|---|---|---|
| Aogami #2 | 1.05–1.15% | W 1.0–1.5%, Cr 0.2–0.5% |
| Shirogami #1 | 1.25–1.35% | none |

- **Edge life:** tungsten carbides are behind blue steel's longer-edge reputation.
- **Sharpening:** that same wear resistance makes blue somewhat slower on the stones ([KSN](https://knifesteelnerds.com/2018/03/29/is-blue-super-steel-actually-super-the-facts-about-tungsten-alloyed-steels/)). White #1 sharpens fastest and gets very keen, which makes it the classic softwood finishing steel.

In a plane, the edge-life gap between White #1 and Blue #2 has never been measured. The smith's heat treatment and your setup matter more.

**Hardness.** Proterial guarantees only "HRC 60 over" after hardening. Kanna makers rarely publish a figure. Good plane blades are generally in the low-to-mid 60s HRC; treat any exact number on a listing as a claim.

**Makers:**
- **Aogami #2:** many of Tsunesaburō's lower-priced planes, and Keizaburō ([Mandaraya](https://daiku-dougu.jp/kizaburou-aogami2gou.html)).
- **Shirogami #1:** for example Yamamoto Kanna's vacuum-melted White #1 "Kinju", 70 mm, $1,020 at Suzuki-ya in Oakland ([Suzuki-ya](https://suzukitool.com/tools/japanese-woodworking-tools/hand-planes/yamamoto/smoothing-hira-ganna/kinju.html)).
- **Swedish carbon steel**, such as Mizuno's ASSAB K120, behaves much like White #1.

Choose by maker and dai rather than by steel label. If anything, White #1 is the friendlier steel to learn sharpening on.

## Q5
Yes, it's a handful.
- Miki's industrial cooperative lists four kanna makers ([cooperative](https://www.miki-kanamono.or.jp/member-list/member_001/001_003_kanna/)).
- The prefecture's tourism site says the town went from about 50 kanna forges at its peak to 4 ([Hyogo tourism](http://www.hyogo-tourism.jp/review/detail_479.html)).
- A 2019–21 survey counted about eight individual kanna smiths in the Miki area, since some workshops have more than one ([Gifu databook](https://www.forest.ac.jp/wp-content/uploads/2021/05/gifu_tools_databook2020.pdf)).
- In the past, Yamamoto's history of the trade counts 21 kanna smiths in 1915, 87 at the 1929 peak, and 55 in 1939 ([Yamamoto](https://yamamotokanna.sakura.ne.jp/mikinokannnakajinorekisibunsyou.html)).

The four makers:
- **Tsunesaburō (常三郎).**
  - Founded 1947; run by the third generation, Uozumi Tōru.
  - The largest and most exported maker, with a dozen-odd steels: Aogami, Shirogami, Swedish (Shion), old-stock Tōgō Rei, and HSS and HAP40 blades that never need tapping out. It also does repairs.
  - The easiest to visit: year-round factory tours by reservation (0794-82-5257, office@tsune36.co.jp), with planes and stones on sale ([tours](https://www.tsune36.co.jp/factory_tour/)).
- **Yamamoto Kanna Seisakusho (山本鉋製作所).**
  - About a century old. The third generation, Yamamoto Yoshihiro (鉄心斎芳楽), and the fourth, Kensuke, are both certified traditional craftsmen.
  - Known for unusual steels: vacuum-melted White #1, Swedish, and the last of the Tōgō No. 0, Tsubame and CWA.
  - Makes everything from tiny planes to ones over 5 sun.
- **Chiyozuru Sadahide (千代鶴貞秀).**
  - Chiyozuru Korehide gave the name to 神吉義良 in 1949. His son Kanki Iwao (b. 1944) became the second Sadahide. Morita Naoki (b. 1978, apprenticed 2004) became the third in February 2019 ([site](https://chiyozurusadahide.jp/shokunin)).
  - Miki's top end: finishing planes such as Rankiku (乱菊), plus kogatana knives.
- **Koyama Kinzoku Kōgyōsho (小山金属工業所).** Founded 1955. It makes chisels, saws and planes under brands such as 松風 and 播磨王, and is more factory than one-man forge.

Apart from Tsunesaburō, contact them well ahead, in Japanese if you can. Most of their sales go through dealers.

## Q6
Ura-gire means the hollow has reached the edge, so lapping alone would just grind away hard steel. Ura-dashi stretches the soft iron so that the steel at the edge bends slightly toward the back. Then you re-lap.

**What to hit**
- Only the soft iron (jigane) on the bevel face.
- Start 2–3 mm behind the line where steel meets iron, and work toward the top of the bevel ([Covington](https://covingtonandsons.com/2021/04/24/sharpening-japanese-tools-part-30-uradashi-uraoshi/)). Mandaraya keeps blows within the upper third of the bevel ([Mandaraya](https://daiku-dougu.jp/kannna-tatakikata.html)).
- Concentrate on the middle third of the width.
- Never strike the hard steel or the ground-back corners. A crack running along the blade can't be fixed; one parallel to the edge can be ground away.

**With what**
- A small, narrow-faced hammer: Kakuri's 15 mm 刃槌, a pointed hammer, or the thin peen of a Warrington.
- Light pecks, often hundreds of them, not force.

**On what**
- An anvil or steel block with a rounded corner; a piece of rail works.
- Put the blade's back on the corner, directly under each blow. A postcard glued to the corner stops slipping.
- Listen: a dull "kotsu-kotsu" means you're over the support; a rattling "kata-kata" means you aren't ([Kakuri](https://www.kakuri.co.jp/2017/01/25/%E9%89%8B%E3%81%AE%E3%81%8A%E6%89%8B%E5%85%A5%E3%82%8C/)).

**Avoiding cracks, and finishing**
- Work in warm weather, or warm the blade first, for example in 30–40 °C water. Cold steel is more brittle ([note](http://mishow-ww.com/wordpress/woodwork/uradashi/)).
- Check often with a straightedge across the edge against a strong light.
- Then lap on a kanaban with carborundum, or on a diamond plate, pressing only near the edge, until the ito-ura is about 0.5–1 mm wide.

**HSS: usually, don't tap it.** Most HSS kanna, including Tsunesaburō's SKH51 and HAP40, have a flat, gently curved back with no hollow and are sold as needing no ura-dashi. Over 90% of Tsunesaburō's HSS planes ship that way ([Tsunesaburō](https://www.tsune36.co.jp/items-service/items003/koganna_skh51/), [Mandaraya](https://daiku-dougu.jp/hisu-kanna-togi.html)). When the back wears, re-lap it flat on a diamond plate; HSS is slow on a kanaban. If yours does have a hollow, ask the maker first. If you must tap, warm the blade and tap the soft iron only, very lightly.

## Q7
Hitachi no longer makes them, but the same steels come from the same mill.
- **The sale.** Hitachi Metals was sold to a Bain Capital-led group and renamed Proterial in January 2023. Yasugi Works and the Yasuki Hagane names (Shirogami, Aogami, Kigami) carried on.
- **The 2026 reorganization.** In April the specialty-steel business moved into Bain's holding company, which took the Proterial name on 1 July. The old company became Proterial Cable Solutions. The steels now sit in an "Aero & Industrial Alloy" division, and the company says it is working toward relisting ([Proterial](https://www.proterial.com/press/2025/pdf/20251215jp.pdf)).

**Aigami (藍紙)** launched on 9 Dec 2025 as a new class, "neither carbon nor stainless" ([Proterial](https://www.proterial.com/press/2025/n1209.html)):
- Aigami No. 2 is in mass production. It is at least as hard as Aogami #2, with rust resistance close to GIN3.
- No. 1 is still in trials.
- The composition is undisclosed.
- The stated uses are kitchen and outdoor knives, razor blades and scalpels; planes aren't mentioned.

**Is white or blue going away?** Officially, no. Proterial's grade pages still list Shirogami 1/2/3, Kigami 2 and Aogami 1/2/Super, with "plane" among the uses ([Aogami 2](https://www.proterial.com/e/yss/search/aogami2.html)). There is no discontinuation notice.

Unofficially, knife forums since 2024 report ([KKF](https://www.kitchenknifeforums.com/threads/are-ao-and-shiro-being-cancelled.72992/)):
- steep price rises: 15–20%, with one claim of 250%
- fewer production runs
- talk of White #3 and yellow being dropped

Much of that is seller talk, and White #3 is still listed. Expect dearer steel rather than no steel, for now.

**An Aigami kanna? Not yet.**
- Its selling point is stain resistance for food knives, which barely matters on an oiled plane blade.
- No plane maker offers it.
- Nobody knows yet how it forge-welds to soft iron or how it behaves in a plane.

Buy Shirogami or Aogami from a good smith, and look again if plane smiths adopt Aigami.

## Q8
Yes, it is still running. The 42nd national meet was held in Miki on 13–14 June 2026. The general division went to 渡辺智紀 Watanabe of Takumi, Itoigawa, who qualified second with a 3.5 µm preliminary shaving ([Takumi](https://takumi-123.com/news/20260625/), [Itoigawa](https://www.city.itoigawa.lg.jp/page/26214.html)).

**Next national meet.** The 43rd is on 21–22 Nov 2026 at Yōkaichiba Dome, Sosa City, Chiba, with about 400 competitors expected ([release](https://www.atpress.ne.jp/news/6487820)). Follow the host committee for news. The national site hasn't been updated since its office moved to Hadano in April 2024 ([kezuroukai.jp](http://kezuroukai.jp/)). Kezurou-kai USA meets in El Cerrito, CA, on 17–18 Oct 2026 ([KK-USA](https://kezuroukai.us/events/)).

**Why the numbers vary.** They come from different rounds, woods and ways of counting.
- **Preliminaries.**
  - Competitors plane their own hinoki board, at least 55 × 1,800 mm, with a 寸八. Each shaving is measured at three points ([rules](https://omotan-hadano.jp/kezuroukai/the40th/competition.html)).
  - Winners get about 3–5 µm; steady good shavings run about 10 µm ([Big Sand](https://www.bigsandwoodworking.com/kezurou-kai-39/)).
  - "2 µm" is the publicity figure. There is no official record list, so treat claims of 1–2 µm as unverified.
- **Finals.**
  - A few finalists plane one board supplied by the organisers, in about five minutes. In 2024 it was local hinoki, 65 mm × 3 m.
  - Winning shavings are far thicker: about 15 µm in 2019 ([KK-USA](https://kezuroukai.us/evolution-of-the-kezurou-contest-in-japan/)), and about 50 µm on quartersawn sugi in 2023 (Big Sand).
- **Three-point totals.** Some reports give the sum of the three points. The 2026 student champion's "19 µm" was such a total, about 6 µm each ([NSG](https://www.nsg.gr.jp/blog/sado_kezuroukai-champion-student_202607/)).

So 2–5 µm is a top preliminary shaving on ideal hinoki. 15–20 µm is a realistic winning finals shaving, or a three-point total.

Neither is shop practice. Competitors arrive with planes already sharp and some tools vacuum-sealed. Judges now limit spreading a shaving to 15 seconds ([Paklan](https://www.paklan.org/a-few-frames-from-kezuroukai-japan-2024/)).
