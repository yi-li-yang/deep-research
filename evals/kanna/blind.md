## Q1

**Question:** I've wanted a Funahiro kanna from Yoita for years and I'm finally ready to order one new, probably a 70 mm Genshin. Is Funatsu-san still taking orders, or has something changed? If I can't get one, who in Yoita is still actually forging kanna that I should look at instead?

### Answer 1

No. You can't order a new Funahiro any more.

Funatsu Yūji (船津祐司, b. 1946) was the third-generation smith behind Funahiro (舟弘). He died on 13 March 2026, according to the dealer [Mandaraya](https://daiku-dougu.jp/). The [Yoita guild](https://yoita-uchihamono.com/) now marks his forge 廃業 (closed).

What's left is dealer stock and the secondhand market:
- [Kurashige](https://shop.kurashige-tools.com/en-us/collections/funahiro) shows every 70 mm Funahiro smoother as sold out. That includes an old-stock Genshin (玄心) #2 that was ¥92,250. Only a 168 mm Genshin large plane (¥473,550) is still listed.
- Hida lists Funahiro planes at $1,007–1,871, all out of stock.

Expect prices to keep rising. If you still want one, watch Japanese dealers' old-stock listings and auctions bought through a proxy.

Yoita itself has shrunk. Three of the guild's five forges have closed: Funahiro, Nakano (chisels) and Usui Hamono. The Yoita knife-craft hall also closed in February 2025. These smiths are still forging:
- **Mizuno Seisuke (水野清介).** He is about 70, a pupil of Usui Kengo and the fourth generation of Mizuno Kanna Seisakusho, with 55 years at the forge.
  - His plane models include 和楽 Waraku, 青壱 Aoichi and 我逢人 Gahōjin. Gahōjin uses ASSAB K120 Swedish steel; Mandaraya listed a 寸八 at ¥49,800, awaiting stock.
  - He also makes large planes (3–10 sun), mame planes and yari-ganna.
  - The [guild page](https://yoita-uchihamono.com/master/) gives his direct phone number for orders. It warns that delivery is slow because he is training apprentices.
  - He is the closest substitute for a Funahiro 寸八.
- **Nitori Tōru (似鳥透, b. 1989).** A former Nikon engineer, he apprenticed with Mizuno from 2020. Since October 2025 he has worked on his own as [Mizudori Hamono (水鳥刃物)](https://mizudori-hamono.com/about/profile/), out of Mizuno's workshop.
  - His 朱雀 Suzaku line has a wrought-iron backing; his 羽根印 line has a mild-steel backing.
  - He is worth a look if you want a wrought-iron-backed plane from the next generation.
- **The Komori brothers (小森).** They make small planes only and have no successor, so they are not a source for a 70 mm smoother.

### Answer 2

I can't confirm Funahiro's current order status. Before you pay for a "new" one, check with a dealer.

What I'm fairly confident of: Funahiro (舟弘) blades come from Funatsu-san's very small workshop in Yoita, which is now part of Nagaoka, Niigata. He is not a young man, and output has been small for years. When something "changes" with a smith in that position, it usually means one of three things:
- His order book has closed.
- He has stopped forging because of age or illness.
- What's being sold is remaining blade stock, fitted to new dai by a dai-maker.

I can't tell you which of these, if any, applies right now. I also can't confirm "Genshin" as a current Funahiro line, so ask exactly what steel and finish you would be getting.

Practical steps:
- Ask a Japanese retailer that has bought from him directly whether new blades are still being forged, or whether they're selling existing stock.
- Search the Japanese second-hand market for 舟弘 寸八 (寸八 is the 70 mm class). Unused old stock and lightly used Funahiro blades do turn up.
- On any used blade, check the remaining length, the ura, and look for cracks at the edge or along the steel/iron line. Budget for re-fitting or replacing the dai.

On who else in Yoita is forging kanna: my understanding is that very few people, if any besides him, still do. Yoita's remaining smiths are better known now for nomi, axes and chōna. I'm not confident naming another Yoita smith who is actively forging plane blades today, and I'd rather not send you after someone who has retired. The local Echigo Yoita uchihamono trade body, or a Niigata tool dealer, can tell you who is actually working. If the answer is "no one," a second-hand Funahiro is your realistic route to a Yoita kanna. Otherwise, widen the search to hand-forging smiths in Miki or Sanjo.

### Answer 3

Yes, something has changed, and it's final. Funatsu Yūji (船津祐司, b. 1946), who forged as Funahiro, died on 13 March 2026. Dealer Risaku Works posts "船津祐司氏は令和8年3月13日にご逝去されました" and warns that new stock will be hard to get ([Risaku Works](https://risakuworks.official.ec/categories/6127805)). The Yoita smiths' association now lists his workshop (船弘刃物製作所) as closed (廃業), with no successor named ([Echigo Yoita Uchihamono](https://yoita-uchihamono.com/)). You can't order one new. A "new" 70 mm Genshin (玄心) can now only be unsold dealer stock, which will be scarce and priced accordingly. Buy only from established dealers.

  He also uses Togo steel and K120. ## Q2

**Question:** Japanese plane catalogs from makers like Tsunesaburo, Yamamoto Kanna and Mizuno list blade steels I can barely find anything about in English: Togo "Ray" steel, Inukubi, Tsubame steel, CWA, and various "Swedish steels" such as Sandvik or ASSAB K120, usually priced well above Aogami. What are these steels, what are they supposed to be good at, and are any of them still being made, or is it all old stock?

### Answer 1

These are mostly legacy or rare steels. The higher prices reflect scarcity and difficult forging more than a proven edge over a well-made Aogami or Shirogami blade.

- **Togo steel (東郷鋼), "Ray" grade (東郷レイ号).**
  - In the late Meiji era, the Tokyo importer Kawai Yōkō Shōten (河合洋鋼商店) had British and Swedish mills make high-carbon, high-tungsten alloy steels. They were first meant for scalpels and metal-cutting tools, and were named after Admiral Tōgō. Japanese sources usually credit a British firm, "Andrew(s)", as the maker.
  - Ray is the grade used for planes. It is hard to forge-weld and a little harder to sharpen, but the edge lasts a very long time ([Yamamoto Kanna steel list](https://yamamotokanna.sakura.ne.jp/siyoukousyu.html), [Japan-Tool](https://www.japan-tool.com/tech_knlg/Togo_Reigou_and_Yokoyama_Kunio.html)).
  - Imports ended by WWII, so only finite old stock remains ([research blog](https://ameblo.jp/seisyutanukikoubou/entry-12920686887.html)).
- **Inukubi (犬首, "dog's-head" stamp).**
  - A related steel from the same Kawai line. Yamamoto says it is close to Togo Ray but even harder to forge and to harden. It doesn't feel hard on the stone and lasts well.
  - Sources disagree on what it is: Tsunesaburo calls its composition HSS-like ([Tsunesaburo](https://www.tsune36.co.jp/kanna_kaitai_shinsho/page_001/)), while Japan-Tool says it is nearer to white steel.
  - Old stock only.
- **CWA (安来CWA).** A Yasugi (Hitachi) tungsten tool steel, roughly equivalent to the old JIS SKS1, at about 1.39% C and 4.65% W. It is a hardwood steel and no longer made (Yamamoto list above).
- **Tsubame (燕/ツバメ鋼).**
  - Made about 40 years ago. Yamamoto says even its analysis is uncertain: modest carbon but plenty of tungsten.
  - It is tough and resists chipping, even on sugi end grain, and is aimed at medium to hard woods ([Honmamon listing](https://www.honmamon.jp/c/gr14/gr33)).
  - I know of no current production, so treat it as stock steel.
- **Swedish steels (Sandvik, ASSAB K120).**
  - Very clean, plain high-carbon steels. K120 is roughly 1.0–1.25% C with a trace of Cr.
  - They behave much like Shirogami #1: easy to sharpen, very keen, with a glossy finish. They do best on softwoods and medium woods ([Tsunesaburo](https://www.tsune36.co.jp/kanna_kaitai_shinsho/page_001/)).
  - Swedish carbon steel is still made. The bars smiths prize, though, are often old stock, such as K120 at "old stock 1.2% carbon" ([Brian Holcombe](https://brianholcombewoodworker.com/guide-to-japanese-chisels/)). Treat "charcoal-smelted, almost tamahagane" sales copy as lore.

"Mizuno" is the Yoita smith in Q1. He uses Togo, Swedish and K120 steel alongside Shirogami #1 and Aogami #1. For scale, Honmamon lists a 70 mm Togo Ray plane at ¥138,000 and a Tsubame one at ¥46,500.

### Answer 2

These steels fall into two groups:
- **Special steels (特殊鋼):** Togo, Inukubi, CWA and Tsubame. They are high-carbon tungsten tool steels, sold for long edge life, mainly in hardwood.
- **Swedish steels:** the opposite. They are clean, plain carbon steels, valued for an easy, keen edge. Only the Swedish steels are clearly still being made.

**Togo (東郷鋼)** is a brand, not one steel. Kawai Yōkō (河合洋鋼) imported these British steels from the Taishō era into early Shōwa and named them after Admiral Tōgō. The maker is usually given as a firm called Andrews. Imports stopped with the war ([notes](https://ameblo.jp/seisyutanukikoubou/entry-12920686887.html)).
- **Rei-gō (レイ号, "Ray")** is a high-carbon tungsten steel, likened to SKS1.
- **Inukubi (犬首)** was sold as "high-speed" steel but is an SKS-class steel. Covington's chart gives it about 5% W plus Cr, Mo and V ([thread](https://sawmillcreek.org/threads/inukubi-steel-plane-blade.204472/)). It only came in narrow bars (about 18 × 4.5 mm), so the ura is narrow.
- Both are hard to forge-weld and reputed to be slow to sharpen. David Weaver, though, found his Inukubi blade easy on natural stones.
- All Togo steel is old stock. Ignore the "fusion of tamahagane and Western steel" sales copy.

**CWA (安来CWA)** was Hitachi's Yasugi version of the old JIS SKS1, at about 1.39% C and 4.65% W, made for hardwood planes ([Mandaraya](https://daiku-dougu.jp/kannna-nitsukawareru-kouzai.html)). It isn't in Proterial's current range, so assume any CWA blade is made from old bar.

**Tsubame (ツバメ/燕鋼)** appeared about 40 years ago as a hardwood-plane steel.
- Its composition was never published. Mandaraya believes it has less carbon and much more tungsten.
- [Tsunesaburō](https://www.tsune36.co.jp/kanna_kaitai_shinsho/page_001/) praises its toughness and very long edge life in hardwood. It wants a blunter bevel and natural finishing stones.
- I couldn't confirm whether it is still produced.

**Swedish steels (Sandvik; ASSAB K120)** are low-impurity carbon steels. Mandaraya likens K120 to Shirogami #1.
- They are easy to sharpen and leave a glossy surface.
- They suit medium to soft woods and wear faster than Aogami.
- They are still current: Tsunesaburō's 汐音 Shion came out in November 2025, and Mizuno makes a K120 plane. Old K120 bar is especially prized.

**Prices.** Tsunesaburō 70 mm planes at [Kurashige](https://shop.kurashige-tools.com/en-us/collections/tsunesaburo-japanese-woodworking-planes):

| Steel | Price |
|---|---|
| Aogami Super | ¥78–81k |
| Swedish (Shion) | ¥71k |
| Tsubame (76 mm) | ¥68k |
| Inukubi | ¥103–108k |
| Togo No. 0 | ¥158,670 |

A plain Tsunesaburō Aogami #1 寸八 costs about ¥27k in Japan. The premium pays for scarcity, forging rejects and fancier models, not for proven performance. No controlled plane test exists for any of these steels. If you buy one, use it on hardwood at a bevel of about 33–35°.

### Answer 3

Most of these aren't modern steel grades you can look up in a mill catalog. They are trade names for particular stocks of steel. Most are old: imported or early Japanese high-carbon steels, known by a brand or by marks stamped on the bar. Smiths bought them decades ago and are working through what's left. In Japan they're often grouped together as "old steels" (古鋼). Reliable compositions are rarely published, and much of what's written about them is lore.

- **Togo "Ray" (東郷鋼):** an old named steel prized for finishing planes. I'm not certain of its origin or what "Ray" refers to, so treat specific origin stories with caution.
- **Inukubi (犬首, "dog's head"), Tsubame (燕, "swallow") and CWA:** these read like the trademark or stamp names old bar stock was known by in the trade. I can't give you trustworthy origins or analyses. They're generally understood to be plain high-carbon steels valued for purity.
- **Swedish steels (Sandvik, ASSAB/Uddeholm):** very clean plain-carbon steels with low phosphorus and sulfur. They have a long reputation in Japan for planes and saws. I'm not sure exactly what grade "K120" denotes.

What they're supposed to be good at:
- taking a very keen edge easily, especially on natural stones
- a resilient edge (粘り, nebari) that resists micro-chipping
- very clean finishing cuts in softwoods like hinoki and sugi

That's why they appear mostly on finishing planes. They usually aren't claimed to beat Aogami for wear resistance. There is little controlled testing behind any of these claims. The smith's forging and heat treatment probably matter as much as the steel, and much of the price premium is rarity and prestige.

Are any still made?
- **Togo, Inukubi, Tsubame and CWA:** as far as I know, these are old stock only, with no current production. Supply is finite and shrinking. You can't verify the steel yourself, so you are relying on the maker's honesty.
- **Swedish carbon steel is the exception.** Swedish mills still make high-purity carbon strip and bar. Sandvik's materials business is now Alleima, and ASSAB distributes Uddeholm steel. So a "Swedish steel" kanna can be new steel, while "old Swedish steel" (古スウェーデン鋼) is sold separately as vintage stock. Ask which one you're paying for.

## Q3

**Question:** I just received my first serious Japanese plane, a 70 mm hira-kanna with a white oak dai, shipped from Japan to me in Arizona. The blade is a very tight fit and the sole looks dead flat out of the box. What should I do in the first few weeks before I start adjusting anything, and then how should I set up the sole and the chipbreaker for finish planing?

### Answer 1

**First few weeks: let it acclimate and don't adjust anything.**
- **Take the wedging pressure off today.** Tap the blade back, or remove the blade and chipbreaker. A white-oak dai going from Japanese humidity into Arizona air will shrink noticeably. A blade that's tight now can split the dai's side walls or crack the mouth as the wood dries around it. Wipe the blade with camellia oil.
- **Slow the drying.** For the first week or two, keep the plane in a paper bag or closed box in the room where it will live, then leave it out. Keep it away from sun, HVAC vents, swamp coolers and hot cars.
- **Track it by weight.** Weigh the dai on a 0.1 g scale every few days. When the weight stops dropping, it has acclimated; that often takes 3–6 weeks in a desert climate. Watch for checks at the ends.
- **Ignore the "dead flat" sole for now.** It will move as the wood dries, and it changes again once the blade is tensioned. It will also shift between monsoon and dry months.

**Then, in this order:**
1. **Fit the blade.** Rub pencil graphite on the blade's front face and along the edges of the back where the grooves bear. Tap it in, withdraw it, and pare the marked high spots on the bed and in the side grooves (osae-mizo), a little at a time. Aim for even bearing. Under hand pressure the blade should stop a few millimetres short of the mouth, then reach cutting depth with a few moderate taps.
2. **True the sole with the blade in.** Set the blade and chipbreaker at working tension with the edge just retracted, because the wedge pressure bends the dai. Check lengthwise and on both diagonals (for twist) with a trustworthy straightedge. Correct it with a dai-naoshi kanna or a card scraper, not sandpaper, which rounds the sole.
3. **Shape the sole for finishing.** Leave three narrow contact lands: both ends of the sole, plus a strip immediately ahead of the edge (the mouth lip). Relieve the areas between them by only a few hundredths of a millimetre, just enough to see a sliver of light under the straightedge. Keep the sole straight across its width.
4. **Set up the chipbreaker (uragane).** Hone a small, steep bevel on its leading edge and polish the top just behind it. It must bear on the blade's back across the full width, with no light showing. Tap the bent ears so it doesn't rock and is held firmly under the pin. For finish work, set it about 0.1 mm behind the edge and parallel to it. Go closer on tear-out-prone grain, and a bit further back on clean softwood.

Re-check the sole often. On a finishing plane, dai-naoshi (truing the sole) is routine maintenance, not a one-time job.

### Answer 2

Expect the dai to move. White oak that left Japan at ordinary humidity will shrink in Arizona air, and a blade that is already tight is exactly what splits the cheeks of a drying dai.

**First 2–4 weeks**
- Take the blade out, or at least tap it well back. For planes moving from humid Japan to a dry climate, Covington advises removing the blade and letting the body acclimatize for a week or so ([Covington](https://covingtonandsons.com/2021/05/08/japanese-handplanes-part-4-fitting-blade-body/)). In Arizona I'd allow longer. Brian Holcombe lets a new dai sit for a few weeks and sets it up only once it stops moving ([Holcombe](https://brianholcombewoodworker.com/2016/08/11/kanna-dai-prep/)).
- Keep the plane in the shop, away from sun, heaters, cooler or AC drafts, and hot cars. Check the sole weekly with a straightedge and winding sticks.
- Use the waiting time on the blade: lap the back, sharpen it, and fit the chipbreaker.
- Once the dai stops changing, refit the blade. A good fit goes in with about three medium mallet blows and adjusts in three or four taps (Covington). If it's still too tight, mark the contact spots with pencil and pare the grooves a hair at a time. Pare sparingly, because monsoon humidity will swell the dai again.
- From then on, store the plane with the blade in place but retracted just above the sole ([Tsunesaburo](https://www.tsune36.co.jp/kanna_kaitai_shinsho/page_005/)).

**Sole for a finish plane**
- Always work the sole with the blade and chipbreaker installed, set just shy of cutting. Blade pressure pushes the sole out around the mouth ([Kakuri](https://www.kakuri.co.jp/2017/01/25/%E9%89%8B%E3%81%AE%E3%81%8A%E6%89%8B%E5%85%A5%E3%82%8C/)).
- Leave two narrow bearing lands: one at the front and one just ahead of the mouth. Relieve everything else, especially behind the mouth.
- A finisher needs only a hair of relief, enough for a thin line of light under a straightedge ([Giant Cypress](https://giantcypress.net/tagged/Japanese%20plane%20setup)). Coarser planes get up to about 0.2 mm ([example](https://hajityoro.com/mokkou10-kannadai-sitaba)).
- Some people prefer the mouth plus the heel instead (Holcombe). Pick one layout and stay consistent.
- Cut the relief with a dai-naoshi scraper plane or a card scraper, and recheck the sole before each session.

**Chipbreaker**
- Its edge should seat on the blade with no light showing across the full width, with a narrow, steep micro-bevel.
- Tap or bend the ears until it needs a few firm taps to seat and presses at the edge instead of rocking on the ears (Kakuri).
- For finishing, start about 0.1–0.2 mm behind the edge. Move it closer only until tear-out stops; set too close, it adds resistance and clogs.

### Answer 3

**First 2–3 weeks: let the body acclimatise, and don't adjust anything.** The white oak body (dai) left Japan at around 60–80% RH. Indoor air in Arizona is often 10–30%, so the body will shrink, possibly by a millimetre or more across its width. A tight blade fights that shrinkage, and that is how dai split in dry climates.
- **Tap the blade and chipbreaker out.** Makers often store planes with the blade merely retracted; Covington stores them out. For a move this drastic, take it out.
- **Slow the change.** For the first few days, keep the plane in its box or a loosely closed bag. That's a sensible precaution, not a tested rule. Then leave it in the shop, away from sun, AC vents and car interiors.
- **Wait for it to stop moving.** Check with a straightedge every few days and start once nothing changes. The usual "about a week" is short for Arizona.
- **Don't trust the "dead flat" sole.** It will move, and a flat sole isn't a finish setup anyway.

**Then set it up in this order.**
1. **Fit the blade (shikomi).**
   - Planes ship deliberately tight, about "90% finished", and after drying the blade may fit tighter still.
   - Mark the bed with pencil or oil and pare only the shiny spots. Stop when the blade seats with 3–4 firm hammer taps and about 1 mm of side play.
   - Keep hard contact off the bed's front corners, which split ([Covington](https://covingtonandsons.com/2021/05/08/japanese-handplanes-part-4-fitting-blade-body/)).
2. **Fit the chipbreaker (uragane).**
   - Its edge must meet the blade's back with no light showing.
   - For finishing, set it "one hair" back, about 0.1–0.3 mm, and only as close as needed to stop tearout. Too close jams the plane and dulls the surface.
   - If it sits skewed, correct it at the cross-pin (osae-bō), not by bending its ears ([Mandaraya](https://daiku-dougu.jp/kannna-osaebou.html)).
3. **True the sole.**
   - Do it with both irons in at working tension and the blade just retracted. The blade's pressure bulges the sole behind the mouth.
   - Tsunesaburō's finishing pattern bears only on the strip just ahead of the mouth and at the dai-jiri. In Japanese usage, the dai-jiri is the end that leads as you pull.
   - Relieve the rest by no more than a paper's thickness.
   - Kakuri draws the bearing bands differently, but in every version the strip ahead of the mouth must bear.
   - If light shows under the straightedge at a bearing band, you've cut too much.
   - Use a sole-scraping plane (台直し鉋).

After that, don't re-true on a schedule. Re-check when the monsoon humidity arrives or when the plane misbehaves.

## Q4

**Question:** For my first finishing kanna I've narrowed it down to Shirogami #1 or Aogami #3. My reasoning is that Aogami #3, being the lowest-carbon blue steel, should be easier to sharpen than Aogami #1 or #2 while still holding an edge longer than white steel. Is that right? What hardness does Aogami #3 usually run at, and which makers offer kanna in it?

### Answer 1

One correction first: **there is no Aogami #3.** Hitachi, now Proterial, makes three blue steels:

| Steel | Carbon |
|---|---|
| Aogami #1 | 1.25–1.35% |
| Aogami #2 | 1.05–1.15% |
| Aogami Super | 1.40–1.50%, plus more W and some V |

The "#3" grades are Shirogami #3 (0.80–0.90% C) and Gingami #3, which is stainless. A blade sold as "Aogami #3" is mislabelled or uses a shop's own name, so ask what it really is.

That turns your reasoning around. The lowest-carbon blue steel is Aogami #2, and it has *less* carbon than Shirogami #1.
- **What separates the two families** is the chromium and tungsten in blue steel. They make heat treatment more forgiving and add some wear resistance.
- **Shirogami** is the purer steel, prized for a very keen edge on natural stones. Its quench window is narrower, so the smith matters more.
- **Knife data show a modest gap.** One retailer's summary of test-based ratings scores Blue #2 5.5 and White #2 4.5 for edge retention, with toughness about equal ([summary](https://www.knivesandstones.com.au/pages/white-vs-blue-steel)). Knife Steel Nerds found Aogami Super less tough than 26C3, which is close to Shirogami #1.
- **There is no controlled test in a plane.** The smith's heat treatment, the dai and your sharpening matter more than the steel.

**Hardness:** plane makers rarely publish it. Blades in either steel generally run in the low to mid 60s HRC. Dictum, for example, quotes 63 HRC for the Aogami blades it fits ([Dictum](https://www.dictum.com/en/dictum-smoothing-plane-no.-4-sk4-blade/703331)).

**Who makes 70 mm (寸八) planes in these steels:**
- **Aogami #1** is the mainstream premium plane steel.
  - Tsunesaburō: its 土性骨 sells for about ¥26,760 in Japan, and Hida sells its Tsunesaburō "Suika" in Aogami for $410.90.
  - Yamamoto Kanna: its 鉄心斎芳楽 sells for about ¥31,000 in Japan.
  - Chiyozuru Sadahide: Aogami #1 is his standard steel.
  - Mizuno Seisuke: his 青壱 Aoichi is often sold out.
- **Aogami #2:** Keizaburō and others.
- **Shirogami #1:** widely available.

Either steel makes a fine first finishing plane, so choose the maker and the dai first.
- Pick **Shirogami #1** if you'll sharpen on natural stones and plane mostly softwood.
- Pick **Aogami #1 or #2** if you want more forgiving heat treatment, or you plane mixed woods.

### Answer 2

Your reasoning has a snag: there is no Aogami #3. ## Q6

**Question:** After a couple of years of sharpening, the flat on the back of my kanna blade has almost disappeared right at the edge (ura-gire). How do I do ura-dashi properly without cracking the blade: what exactly do I hit, with what, and on what? I also have a newer HSS kanna. When its back wears, should I tap that one out the same way?

### Answer 1

Ura-dashi works by stretching the soft iron so the hard steel bows toward the back. Success depends on two things: never touching the steel, and always backing up each blow.

- **Warm the blade first** when it's cool, to body temperature or in warm water. Cold steel cracks more easily ([daiku-dougu](https://daiku-dougu.jp/kannna-tatakikata.html)).
- **What to hit:** only the soft iron (jigane) on the bevel side.
  - Covington marks a band that starts 2–3 mm back from the steel/iron line and runs to the end of the iron.
  - About two-thirds of the blows go in the middle third of the width, staying about 3 mm off the sides ([Covington](https://covingtonandsons.com/2021/04/24/sharpening-japanese-tools-part-30-uradashi-uraoshi/)).
  - Kakuri gives the same rule: tap the middle of the soft iron, and expect the blade to crack if you strike the hard steel at the edge ([Kakuri](https://www.kakuri.co.jp/2017/01/25/%E9%89%8B%E3%81%AE%E3%81%8A%E6%89%8B%E5%85%A5%E3%82%8C/)).
- **With what:** a small, light hammer with a small face. Options are a ~15 mm hazuchi (刃槌) or pointed hammer (Kakuri), the pointed end of a small genno, or the peen of a Warrington or tack hammer (Covington). Use many precise light taps, not a few heavy ones ([JTAS](https://www.japanesetools.com.au/blogs/jtas-plane-tips/tuning-japanese-planes-addendum-b-ura-dashi-tapping)).
- **On what:** a heavy steel anvil with a rounded corner or crowned face, such as a rail section, a kanatoko or a 目振り台.
  - Lay the blade back-down with the anvil directly under the spot you strike.
  - A good blow sounds solid, not rattly. Unsupported blows are what crack blades.
- **Check often:** sight a straightedge against the light, or take a few strokes on the kanaban. Stop once the edge zone stands proud.
- **Then ura-oshi:**
  - Use a kanaban with a little carborundum. Kakuri suggests about half a teaspoon and three drops of water.
  - Keep the pressure near the edge, so you restore a thin land and keep the hollow.
  - Then move to fine stones.

**HSS:** generally, don't tap it out. Many HSS kanna are built so they never need it. Tsunesaburo sells its SKH51 and HAP40 lines as ura-dashi-free, with no side lands (a gentle curve toward the edge) or no hollow at all. On those you simply hone the back again; a diamond plate makes that much faster ([SKH51](https://www.tsune36.co.jp/items-service/items003/koganna_skh51/), [HAP40](https://ad-tools.com.au/products/tsunesaburo-hap40-hss-kanna)). If yours has a conventional hollow back, ask the maker before hammering, because HSS is far less forgiving than carbon steel.

### Answer 2

Losing the flat at the edge (ura-gire) after a couple of years is normal. The safest fix comes first: if the loss is small, grind the bevel back a millimetre or two until the flat reaches the edge again. It costs blade length but carries no risk.

**Ura-dashi (tapping out):**
- **With what:** a small blade hammer (刃槌) with a face of about 15 mm, slightly crowned, or a small pointed metalworking hammer. Use light, controlled taps, not blows ([Kakuri](https://ameblo.jp/kakuri-blog/entry-12163338332.html)).
- **On what:** an anvil or hard steel block with a rounded corner (目振り台), fixed solidly to a heavy wooden base.
- **Where to hit:**
  - Lay the blade back-down. Put the rounded corner directly under the spot you'll strike, near the edge and seated in the hollow, so the back is fully supported.
  - Strike only the soft iron (jigane) on the bevel side, starting about 2–3 mm behind the line where the hard steel shows. Never hit the steel itself.
  - Work across the width, concentrating where the flat has gone.
- **Listen:** a dull "kotsu-kotsu" means the blade is supported. A sharp, clattering sound means there's a gap, so stop and reposition. Unsupported blows crack the steel. A hidden crack can later let a 3–4 mm chunk of edge break off as you sharpen down ([Mandaraya](https://daiku-dougu.jp/10-4.htm)).
- **In cold weather:** warm the blade to body temperature first.
- **Check often:** every few taps, rub the back on a stone to see where it bears. Stop as soon as you can restore a continuous flat strip at the edge (ito-ura) about 0.5–1 mm wide. Overdoing it widens the flat.
- **Then lap it (ura-oshi):** a kanaban with carborundum is traditional. Flat stones or a diamond plate do the job more consistently.

**Your HSS kanna: don't tap it.** [Tsunesaburō's HSS blades](https://www.tsune36.co.jp/items-service/items003/koganna_skh51/) are built never to need ura-dashi. They are HSS on very soft iron, and the back has no hollow "legs", only a gentle curve from the centre to the edge.
- When the back wears, lap it on a flat stone or diamond plate, or grind the bevel back.
- HSS cuts slowly on natural stones and is too brittle to hammer.
- If your HSS blade is from another maker and has a hollow back, ask that maker before trying anything.

### Answer 3

Ura-dashi works by stretching the soft iron (jigane) so the edge region bends very slightly toward the back. You then lap the back (ura-oshi) to bring the flat strip along the edge (ito-ura) back.

**What to hit:** the soft-iron part of the bevel (kiriha), close to the line where the hard steel begins but never on the steel. Also avoid the edge itself and the corners. Start in the middle of the width and work outward evenly, staying a few millimetres in from each side.

**What to hit it with:** a small hammer of roughly 100–200 g with a slightly domed face, or a dedicated ura-dashi hammer with a narrow rounded peen. Don't use your regular gennō swung hard.

**What to rest it on:** a small anvil or a solid hardened steel block on a firm bench, with the blade back-down. The critical point is that the back must be supported directly under each blow. Because of the hollow (ura-suki), the spot under the blow may not be touching a flat anvil. Tilt the blade, or use the anvil's edge, so the back bears right there. Hammering an unsupported blade is how blades crack. A ringing note usually means the blade is unsupported; a dull "tok" means it's bearing.

**Technique:**
- In cold weather, warm the blade first until it feels comfortably warm. Cold hard steel is more brittle.
- Use light, controlled taps in rows across the width, not a few heavy blows.
- After every few taps, rub the back briefly on a flat kanaban or diamond plate and see where the bright strip appears. Stop once it runs the full width at the edge.
- Then do a proper ura-oshi on a flat plate with carborundum, or on a coarse diamond plate. Use a stick (ura-oshi bō) to keep pressure near the edge without rounding it, then finish on your stones.
- Doing a little often is better than waiting until the ura-gire is severe.

**The HSS kanna:** no, don't peen it. High-speed steel tolerates hammering far less well. Many HSS blades also aren't the traditional forge-welded laminate of steel on soft iron that ura-dashi relies on. Most makers say not to tap them out, but check what yours says. Instead, restore the flat by lapping the back on a coarse diamond plate. The hollow will shrink over time, so lap the back more often. When the hollow is nearly gone, the maker or a professional sharpener can re-grind it.

The spread from 2 to 20 microns mostly comes from comparing different things:
- **Elite results at the national meets:** continuous, full-width hinoki shavings measured with a micrometer. In recent years the winning shavings have generally been reported at roughly 3–6 µm.
- **Claims of 2 µm or less:** these exist, but a standard micrometer can barely resolve that on a shaving that compresses. Treat them as the single thinnest reading, not a typical shaving.
- **Around 10 µm:** an excellent result for a strong amateur, and typical of many overseas event winners.
- **15–20 µm:** good work by most standards. Figures like these often come from harder or less ideal wood, early rounds, or casual demonstrations.

The numbers also depend on how the shaving was made and measured:
- **Wood and preparation:** clear, straight-grained, moisture-conditioned hinoki is far easier than anything else.
- **Humidity.**
- **What the figure means:** the thinnest point or an average along the shaving.
- **The micrometer:** shavings compress, so the instrument and how hard its jaws close matter.
- **The rules:** whether the shaving had to be unbroken over the full length and width.

I'm not certain of the most recent winning figures, so take 3–6 µm as the recent elite range rather than a precise record.

Yes, Kezurou-kai is still running. The 40th national meet was in Hadano (Nov 2024, 700+ competitors), the 41st in Katano (Apr 2025) and the 42nd in Miki (13–14 Jun 2026).

**The next national meet is the 43rd: Sosa City, Chiba, 21–22 Nov 2026, at the Yōkaichiba Dome (八日市場ドーム).** The organisers expect about 400 competitors and 5,000 visitors ([press release](https://www.atpress.ne.jp/news/6487820)). The national body's [own site](https://kezuroukai.jp/) hasn't been updated since 2024 and money is tight, so follow the local committee's announcements. In the US, [Kezurou-kai USA](https://kezuroukai.us/events/) meets in El Cerrito, CA, on 17–18 Oct 2026.

The numbers you've seen vary because they come from different rounds and different boards:
- **Preliminary thin-shaving round:** competitors bring their own hinoki board (at least 55 × 1,800 mm), the plane must be under 76 mm, and each shaving is measured at three points ([rules](https://kezuroukai-katano.to-ryou.com/competition/)).
  - Winners typically take **3–4 µm**.
  - At Miki in 2026, the general-division winner, 渡辺智紀 of Itoigawa (read as Watanabe Tomoki; reading unconfirmed), took **3.5 µm twice** in the preliminaries. That put him second, and he went on to win the final ([his company's note](https://takumi-123.com/news/20260625/)).
  - 2 µm shavings have been pinned up since at least 2009, and "2 microns" is the headline in the Sosa promotion.
- **Finals:** these use boards the organisers supply, and shavings are much thicker, about **15–50 µm**. That's where the "nearly 20" figures come from.
- **There is no official record list.** Claims of 1 µm are unverified.

Sub-10 µm planing is a contest skill:
- Competitors bring pre-sharpened planes, vacuum-seal their tools and boards, and keep the boards damp.
- They favour single-blade bodies with tight mouths.
- Judges allow 15 seconds to spread a shaving before it is measured.

As one observer put it, the control involved is "far from the reality of day-to-day work" ([Paklan](https://www.paklan.org/a-few-frames-from-kezuroukai-japan-2024/)).
