## Q1
No. You can't order a new Funahiro any more.

Funatsu Yūji (船津祐司, b. 1946) was the third-generation smith behind Funahiro (舟弘). He died on 13 March 2026, according to the dealer [Mandaraya](https://daiku-dougu.jp/). The [Yoita guild](https://yoita-uchihamono.com/) now marks his forge 廃業 (closed).

What's left is dealer stock and the secondhand market:
- [Kurashige](https://shop.kurashige-tools.com/en-us/collections/funahiro) shows every 70 mm Funahiro smoother as sold out. That includes an old-stock Genshin (玄心) #2 that was ¥92,250. Only a 168 mm Genshin large plane (¥473,550) is still listed.
- Hida lists Funahiro planes at $1,007–1,871, all out of stock.

Expect prices to keep rising. If you still want one, watch Japanese dealers' old-stock listings and auctions bought through a proxy.

Yoita itself has shrunk. Three of the guild's five forges have closed: Funahiro, Nakano (chisels) and Usui Hamono. The Yoita knife-craft hall also closed in February 2025. These smiths are still forging:
- **Mizuno Seisuke (水野清介).** He is about 70, a pupil of Usui Kengo and the fourth generation of Mizuno Kanna Seisakusho, with 55 years at the forge.
  - His plane models include 和楽 Waraku, 青壱 Aoichi and 我逢人 Gahōjin. Gahōjin uses ASSAB K120 Swedish steel; Mandaraya listed a 寸八 at ¥49,800, awaiting stock.
  - He also makes large planes (3–10 sun), mame planes and yari-ganna.
  - The [guild page](https://yoita-uchihamono.com/master/) gives his direct phone number for orders. It warns that delivery is slow because he is training apprentices.
  - He is the closest substitute for a Funahiro 寸八.
- **Nitori Tōru (似鳥透, b. 1989).** A former Nikon engineer, he apprenticed with Mizuno from 2020. Since October 2025 he has worked on his own as [Mizudori Hamono (水鳥刃物)](https://mizudori-hamono.com/about/profile/), out of Mizuno's workshop.
  - His 朱雀 Suzaku line has a wrought-iron backing; his 羽根印 line has a mild-steel backing.
  - He is worth a look if you want a wrought-iron-backed plane from the next generation.
- **The Komori brothers (小森).** They make small planes only and have no successor, so they are not a source for a 70 mm smoother.

## Q2
These steels fall into two groups:
- **Special steels (特殊鋼):** Togo, Inukubi, CWA and Tsubame. They are high-carbon tungsten tool steels, sold for long edge life, mainly in hardwood.
- **Swedish steels:** the opposite. They are clean, plain carbon steels, valued for an easy, keen edge. Only the Swedish steels are clearly still being made.

**Togo (東郷鋼)** is a brand, not one steel. Kawai Yōkō (河合洋鋼) imported these British steels from the Taishō era into early Shōwa and named them after Admiral Tōgō. The maker is usually given as a firm called Andrews. Imports stopped with the war ([notes](https://ameblo.jp/seisyutanukikoubou/entry-12920686887.html)).
- **Rei-gō (レイ号, "Ray")** is a high-carbon tungsten steel, likened to SKS1.
- **Inukubi (犬首)** was sold as "high-speed" steel but is an SKS-class steel. Covington's chart gives it about 5% W plus Cr, Mo and V ([thread](https://sawmillcreek.org/threads/inukubi-steel-plane-blade.204472/)). It only came in narrow bars (about 18 × 4.5 mm), so the ura is narrow.
- Both are hard to forge-weld and reputed to be slow to sharpen. David Weaver, though, found his Inukubi blade easy on natural stones.
- All Togo steel is old stock. Ignore the "fusion of tamahagane and Western steel" sales copy.

**CWA (安来CWA)** was Hitachi's Yasugi version of the old JIS SKS1, at about 1.39% C and 4.65% W, made for hardwood planes ([Mandaraya](https://daiku-dougu.jp/kannna-nitsukawareru-kouzai.html)). It isn't in Proterial's current range, so assume any CWA blade is made from old bar.

**Tsubame (ツバメ/燕鋼)** appeared about 40 years ago as a hardwood-plane steel.
- Its composition was never published. Mandaraya believes it has less carbon and much more tungsten.
- [Tsunesaburō](https://www.tsune36.co.jp/kanna_kaitai_shinsho/page_001/) praises its toughness and very long edge life in hardwood. It wants a blunter bevel and natural finishing stones.
- I couldn't confirm whether it is still produced.

**Swedish steels (Sandvik; ASSAB K120)** are low-impurity carbon steels. Mandaraya likens K120 to Shirogami #1.
- They are easy to sharpen and leave a glossy surface.
- They suit medium to soft woods and wear faster than Aogami.
- They are still current: Tsunesaburō's 汐音 Shion came out in November 2025, and Mizuno makes a K120 plane. Old K120 bar is especially prized.

**Prices.** Tsunesaburō 70 mm planes at [Kurashige](https://shop.kurashige-tools.com/en-us/collections/tsunesaburo-japanese-woodworking-planes):

| Steel | Price |
|---|---|
| Aogami Super | ¥78–81k |
| Swedish (Shion) | ¥71k |
| Tsubame (76 mm) | ¥68k |
| Inukubi | ¥103–108k |
| Togo No. 0 | ¥158,670 |

A plain Tsunesaburō Aogami #1 寸八 costs about ¥27k in Japan. The premium pays for scarcity, forging rejects and fancier models, not for proven performance. No controlled plane test exists for any of these steels. If you buy one, use it on hardwood at a bevel of about 33–35°.

## Q3
**First 2–3 weeks: let the body acclimatise, and don't adjust anything.** The white oak body (dai) left Japan at around 60–80% RH. Indoor air in Arizona is often 10–30%, so the body will shrink, possibly by a millimetre or more across its width. A tight blade fights that shrinkage, and that is how dai split in dry climates.
- **Tap the blade and chipbreaker out.** Makers often store planes with the blade merely retracted; Covington stores them out. For a move this drastic, take it out.
- **Slow the change.** For the first few days, keep the plane in its box or a loosely closed bag. That's a sensible precaution, not a tested rule. Then leave it in the shop, away from sun, AC vents and car interiors.
- **Wait for it to stop moving.** Check with a straightedge every few days and start once nothing changes. The usual "about a week" is short for Arizona.
- **Don't trust the "dead flat" sole.** It will move, and a flat sole isn't a finish setup anyway.

**Then set it up in this order.**
1. **Fit the blade (shikomi).**
   - Planes ship deliberately tight, about "90% finished", and after drying the blade may fit tighter still.
   - Mark the bed with pencil or oil and pare only the shiny spots. Stop when the blade seats with 3–4 firm hammer taps and about 1 mm of side play.
   - Keep hard contact off the bed's front corners, which split ([Covington](https://covingtonandsons.com/2021/05/08/japanese-handplanes-part-4-fitting-blade-body/)).
2. **Fit the chipbreaker (uragane).**
   - Its edge must meet the blade's back with no light showing.
   - For finishing, set it "one hair" back, about 0.1–0.3 mm, and only as close as needed to stop tearout. Too close jams the plane and dulls the surface.
   - If it sits skewed, correct it at the cross-pin (osae-bō), not by bending its ears ([Mandaraya](https://daiku-dougu.jp/kannna-osaebou.html)).
3. **True the sole.**
   - Do it with both irons in at working tension and the blade just retracted. The blade's pressure bulges the sole behind the mouth.
   - Tsunesaburō's finishing pattern bears only on the strip just ahead of the mouth and at the dai-jiri. In Japanese usage, the dai-jiri is the end that leads as you pull.
   - Relieve the rest by no more than a paper's thickness.
   - Kakuri draws the bearing bands differently, but in every version the strip ahead of the mouth must bear.
   - If light shows under the straightedge at a bearing band, you've cut too much.
   - Use a sole-scraping plane (台直し鉋).

After that, don't re-true on a schedule. Re-check when the monsoon humidity arrives or when the plane misbehaves.

## Q4
One correction first: **there is no Aogami #3.** Hitachi, now Proterial, makes three blue steels:

| Steel | Carbon |
|---|---|
| Aogami #1 | 1.25–1.35% |
| Aogami #2 | 1.05–1.15% |
| Aogami Super | 1.40–1.50%, plus more W and some V |

The "#3" grades are Shirogami #3 (0.80–0.90% C) and Gingami #3, which is stainless. A blade sold as "Aogami #3" is mislabelled or uses a shop's own name, so ask what it really is.

That turns your reasoning around. The lowest-carbon blue steel is Aogami #2, and it has *less* carbon than Shirogami #1.
- **What separates the two families** is the chromium and tungsten in blue steel. They make heat treatment more forgiving and add some wear resistance.
- **Shirogami** is the purer steel, prized for a very keen edge on natural stones. Its quench window is narrower, so the smith matters more.
- **Knife data show a modest gap.** One retailer's summary of test-based ratings scores Blue #2 5.5 and White #2 4.5 for edge retention, with toughness about equal ([summary](https://www.knivesandstones.com.au/pages/white-vs-blue-steel)). Knife Steel Nerds found Aogami Super less tough than 26C3, which is close to Shirogami #1.
- **There is no controlled test in a plane.** The smith's heat treatment, the dai and your sharpening matter more than the steel.

**Hardness:** plane makers rarely publish it. Blades in either steel generally run in the low to mid 60s HRC. Dictum, for example, quotes 63 HRC for the Aogami blades it fits ([Dictum](https://www.dictum.com/en/dictum-smoothing-plane-no.-4-sk4-blade/703331)).

**Who makes 70 mm (寸八) planes in these steels:**
- **Aogami #1** is the mainstream premium plane steel.
  - Tsunesaburō: its 土性骨 sells for about ¥26,760 in Japan, and Hida sells its Tsunesaburō "Suika" in Aogami for $410.90.
  - Yamamoto Kanna: its 鉄心斎芳楽 sells for about ¥31,000 in Japan.
  - Chiyozuru Sadahide: Aogami #1 is his standard steel.
  - Mizuno Seisuke: his 青壱 Aoichi is often sold out.
- **Aogami #2:** Keizaburō and others.
- **Shirogami #1:** widely available.

Either steel makes a fine first finishing plane, so choose the maker and the dai first.
- Pick **Shirogami #1** if you'll sharpen on natural stones and plane mostly softwood.
- Pick **Aogami #1 or #2** if you want more forgiving heat treatment, or you plane mixed woods.

  - His standard steel is Aogami #1. His top planes use inherited special Yasugi steel and old Swedish steel.

## Q6
Losing the flat at the edge (ura-gire) after a couple of years is normal. The safest fix comes first: if the loss is small, grind the bevel back a millimetre or two until the flat reaches the edge again. It costs blade length but carries no risk.

**Ura-dashi (tapping out):**
- **With what:** a small blade hammer (刃槌) with a face of about 15 mm, slightly crowned, or a small pointed metalworking hammer. Use light, controlled taps, not blows ([Kakuri](https://ameblo.jp/kakuri-blog/entry-12163338332.html)).
- **On what:** an anvil or hard steel block with a rounded corner (目振り台), fixed solidly to a heavy wooden base.
- **Where to hit:**
  - Lay the blade back-down. Put the rounded corner directly under the spot you'll strike, near the edge and seated in the hollow, so the back is fully supported.
  - Strike only the soft iron (jigane) on the bevel side, starting about 2–3 mm behind the line where the hard steel shows. Never hit the steel itself.
  - Work across the width, concentrating where the flat has gone.
- **Listen:** a dull "kotsu-kotsu" means the blade is supported. A sharp, clattering sound means there's a gap, so stop and reposition. Unsupported blows crack the steel. A hidden crack can later let a 3–4 mm chunk of edge break off as you sharpen down ([Mandaraya](https://daiku-dougu.jp/10-4.htm)).
- **In cold weather:** warm the blade to body temperature first.
- **Check often:** every few taps, rub the back on a stone to see where it bears. Stop as soon as you can restore a continuous flat strip at the edge (ito-ura) about 0.5–1 mm wide. Overdoing it widens the flat.
- **Then lap it (ura-oshi):** a kanaban with carborundum is traditional. Flat stones or a diamond plate do the job more consistently.

**Your HSS kanna: don't tap it.** [Tsunesaburō's HSS blades](https://www.tsune36.co.jp/items-service/items003/koganna_skh51/) are built never to need ura-dashi. They are HSS on very soft iron, and the back has no hollow "legs", only a gentle curve from the centre to the edge.
- When the back wears, lap it on a flat stone or diamond plate, or grind the bevel back.
- HSS cuts slowly on natural stones and is too brittle to hammer.
- If your HSS blade is from another maker and has a hollow back, ask that maker before trying anything.

## Q7
Hitachi no longer owns the business, but the same steels still come from the same Yasugi works.

**What changed:**
- A Bain Capital-led group took Hitachi Metals private, and it was renamed **Proterial on 4 Jan 2023**.
- A restructuring took effect on **1 Jul 2026**. Bain's acquisition company itself now trades as Proterial, Ltd. and holds the core businesses, including the YSS steels in its Aero & Industrial Alloy unit.
- The company is preparing to list on the stock market again ([Nikkei](https://www.nikkei.com/article/DGXZQOUC1568I0V11C25A2000000/)).

**Supply:**
- Proterial still lists 青紙 Aogami, 黄紙 Kigami and 白紙 Shirogami ([product page](https://www.proterial.com/products/tool_steel/highclass_cutlery_steels.html)) and has published no discontinuation notice. Its catalog sits behind a request form.
- Dealers and forums talk of a roughly 250% price rise and the loss of Shirogami #3 and Kigami. Proterial hasn't confirmed either, and talk of scarcity also helps sell tools.
- **Nothing official suggests that Shirogami or Aogami for plane blades is going away.** The real bottleneck is smiths, not steel.

**Aigami (藍紙)** was [announced on 9 Dec 2025](https://www.proterial.com/press/2025/n1209.html) as a steel that is "neither carbon nor stainless". It comes in two grades:
- **No. 1** aims at corrosion resistance and rusts about as little as SUS420J2.
- **No. 2** aims at hardness. It is at least as hard as Aogami #2, with rust resistance approaching Gin-3. Only No. 2 is in mass production.

Its stated uses are kitchen and outdoor knives, razors and scalpels. Planes and chisels aren't mentioned.

**Should you look for an Aigami kanna? No, not now.**
- No plane maker appears to offer one.
- Its main selling point, stain resistance, matters little for a plane blade you keep oiled.
- Nobody has published how it forge-welds to jigane or how it performs in a plane.

Buy Shirogami or Aogami, and look at Aigami again once smiths have had a few years with it.

## Q8
Yes, Kezurou-kai is still running. The 40th national meet was in Hadano (Nov 2024, 700+ competitors), the 41st in Katano (Apr 2025) and the 42nd in Miki (13–14 Jun 2026).

**The next national meet is the 43rd: Sosa City, Chiba, 21–22 Nov 2026, at the Yōkaichiba Dome (八日市場ドーム).** The organisers expect about 400 competitors and 5,000 visitors ([press release](https://www.atpress.ne.jp/news/6487820)). The national body's [own site](https://kezuroukai.jp/) hasn't been updated since 2024 and money is tight, so follow the local committee's announcements. In the US, [Kezurou-kai USA](https://kezuroukai.us/events/) meets in El Cerrito, CA, on 17–18 Oct 2026.

The numbers you've seen vary because they come from different rounds and different boards:
- **Preliminary thin-shaving round:** competitors bring their own hinoki board (at least 55 × 1,800 mm), the plane must be under 76 mm, and each shaving is measured at three points ([rules](https://kezuroukai-katano.to-ryou.com/competition/)).
  - Winners typically take **3–4 µm**.
  - At Miki in 2026, the general-division winner, 渡辺智紀 of Itoigawa (read as Watanabe Tomoki; reading unconfirmed), took **3.5 µm twice** in the preliminaries. That put him second, and he went on to win the final ([his company's note](https://takumi-123.com/news/20260625/)).
  - 2 µm shavings have been pinned up since at least 2009, and "2 microns" is the headline in the Sosa promotion.
- **Finals:** these use boards the organisers supply, and shavings are much thicker, about **15–50 µm**. That's where the "nearly 20" figures come from.
- **There is no official record list.** Claims of 1 µm are unverified.

Sub-10 µm planing is a contest skill:
- Competitors bring pre-sharpened planes, vacuum-seal their tools and boards, and keep the boards damp.
- They favour single-blade bodies with tight mouths.
- Judges allow 15 seconds to spread a shaving before it is measured.

As one observer put it, the control involved is "far from the reality of day-to-day work" ([Paklan](https://www.paklan.org/a-few-frames-from-kezuroukai-japan-2024/)).
